\begin{abstract}
Repeated computational homogenization becomes expensive when the same
microstructure must be solved for many different local constitutive
properties. This work formulates that setting as a reusable transfer problem
for a fixed periodic microstructure. The formulation applies to linear,
symmetric, and coercive periodic cell problems with separable constitutive
dependence, including thermal and electrical conduction, mass diffusion,
dielectric electrostatics, linear Darcy transport, and linear elasticity.
The independent macroscopic loading problems are assembled into one discrete
Schur map, while material dependence is separated from the fixed geometry
through an affine operator representation. During the offline stage, one
fixed Sobol sequence is traversed with the full-order Fourier--Galerkin model
and the resulting microscopic responses are appended to a common snapshot
matrix. The complete snapshot span is retained without an energy threshold,
numerical-rank truncation, or prescribed reduced dimension. At model freeze,
an exact reference-energy Cholesky--QR transformation normalizes this span
using only the already contracted affine blocks and no additional voxel pass.
Because the raw
snapshot spaces are nested under enrichment, the resulting Ritz--Schur
operators converge monotonically in the Loewner sense in exact arithmetic.
Their difference from the full-order operator is positive semidefinite and
satisfies an exact microscopic energy identity.
Training follows a fixed Sobol prefix and is stopped adaptively using five
independent Fourier--Galerkin monitor materials. Starting from two training
materials, one material is appended at a time until the maximum monitor error
is at most \(10^{-4}\). The frozen model is then assessed once on a second,
independent 20-material maximin design selected from a 4096-point Sobol pool;
its FOM responses have no role in snapshot enrichment, span construction, or
stopping.
Across ten elastic microstructures, the adaptive construction selects between
6 and 16 training materials and full snapshot ranks between 36 and 96. The
wider held-out design exposes a generalization gap: 89 of 200
material--geometry pairs satisfy the \(10^{-4}\) target and 195 satisfy
\(10^{-3}\), with mean and maximum relative tensor errors of
\(2.138\times10^{-4}\) and \(1.306\times10^{-3}\), respectively.
All held-out reduced systems and stiffness tensors remain positive definite.
At the same geometry-wise training counts, the ROM has lower error than
training-only-tuned radial-basis-function and Kriging surrogates for all 200
paired comparisons; their aggregate mean errors are
\(6.293\times10^{-2}\) and \(7.258\times10^{-2}\), respectively.
The online stage uses only reduced dense systems assembled from the
precomputed affine blocks.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item A common transfer formulation covers linear coercive transport and elasticity cell problems.
\item Exact affine separation compiles fixed geometry while queried material enters only through constitutive coefficients.
\item Sobol sampling and reference-energy QR retain the complete snapshot span without truncation.
\item Nested full-span Ritz spaces give monotone Loewner convergence and an exact operator-error energy identity.
\item Same-prefix RBF and Kriging benchmarks demonstrate the sample efficiency of the physics-based transfer space.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Computational homogenization links a heterogeneous microstructure to an
effective macroscopic constitutive or transport response through the solution
of a microscopic boundary-value problem. For a single material configuration,
this cost can be acceptable. In many-query settings, however, the dominant
burden is often the repeated solution of the same resolved microstructure for
many admissible combinations of constituent properties. Such situations arise
in material screening, sensitivity analysis \cite{kaminski2003sensitivity},
uncertainty propagation, inverse identification, constitutive calibration,
high-fidelity database generation, optimization, and nested multiscale
calculations such as FE\textsuperscript{2} \cite{feyel2000fe2}. In these
applications, the geometry of the periodic cell may remain unchanged while
hundreds or thousands of constitutive parameter sets must be evaluated.

For periodic media represented on regular grids, FFT-based methods provide an
efficient full-field route to homogenization. Starting from the classical
scheme of Moulinec and Suquet \cite{moulinec1998numerical}, accelerated
polarization-based iterations \cite{eyre1999fast,michel2001augmented} and
variational Fourier--Galerkin formulations
\cite{vondrejc2014fft,vondrejc2015bounds} have substantially improved the
efficiency, robustness, and mathematical interpretation of periodic cell
solvers. Nevertheless, these advances primarily reduce the cost of an
individual full-order solve. If the microstructure is fixed but its local
constitutive properties are changed repeatedly, each new material point still
requires the solution of a high-dimensional microscopic problem.

Projection-based model reduction addresses this repeated cost by exploiting
the low-dimensional structure of families of microscopic solutions.
Reduced-basis and POD approaches have consequently been developed for
computational homogenization in linear and nonlinear settings
\cite{boyaval2008homogenization,yvonnet2007r3m,fritzen2013hybrid,
hernandez2014highperformance,kunc2019finite}. The most directly related work
is that of Pham and Lee \cite{pham2025rbhm}, which already combines periodic
homogenization, reduced-basis offline--online decomposition, variable
constituent properties, and thermal and elastic effective responses. Thus,
neither the fixed-geometry many-material setting nor the use of a reduced
basis and an offline--online split is claimed as new here.

Several neighboring developments further delimit the present contribution.
Classical reduced-basis methodology established affine offline--online
decomposition and error control for coercive parametrized problems
\cite{prudhomme2002reliable,quarteroni2011certified}, including adaptations to
homogenization and elasticity \cite{kerfriden2014cre,hoang2016twofield}.
Static-condensation reduced-basis methods already treat reduced Schur
complements, port reduction, and certification
\cite{huynh2013scrbe,eftang2013port,smetana2015certification}, while
variationally consistent micromechanical reduction predates the present work
\cite{michel2016variational}. Non-intrusive approaches have
combined microscopic POD information with regression of constitutive
responses under material or geometric variation
\cite{guo2021constitutive,guo2024geometric}. Within FFT homogenization,
sparse sampling, low-rank tensors, and tensor trains reduce spatial solution
or storage cost \cite{kochmann2019sparsefft,vondrejc2020lowrank,hauck2026sfft}.
Sensitivity-based reduction has also been considered for nonlinear
hyperelastic homogenization \cite{lukes2026sensitivity}. Accordingly, POD,
affine parametrization, Schur condensation, and preservation of variational
structure are established ingredients rather than separate novelty claims.

The methodological contribution pursued here is their specific integration
into an FFT-based constitutive compiler for one fully resolved periodic
geometry. The Fourier--Galerkin cell equations for all independent
macroscopic loadings are written as one effective Schur map; the local
constitutive family is separated exactly into reusable geometry-dependent
blocks \(\{K_q,B_q,D_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}\); and those blocks are projected onto the
complete span of a fixed Sobol prefix of microscopic responses. Retaining the
entire snapshot span, rather than selecting an energy fraction or a
prescribed reduced dimension, makes the prefix spaces nested. In this
specific construction, the reduced--full-order Schur difference admits the
exact microscopic energy identity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity}, and
successive prefix operators obey the Loewner ordering in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur}. After compilation, a material query
uses only affine coefficient evaluation, reduced dense assembly, and a small
solve; neither an FFT iteration nor a voxel-scale reconstruction remains in
the deployed query. The contribution therefore lies in this combined
FFT--affine--Ritz--Schur construction and its consequences rather than in any
one of its constituent techniques.

The numerical study then evaluates whether this construction is useful under
a deliberately separated protocol. Five independent FOM materials control
Sobol-prefix enrichment, after which the reduced operators are frozen and a
second maximin design is used only for final assessment. Training-only-tuned
RBF and Kriging models provide same-prefix data-only references. Within the
assumptions of linearity, symmetry, coercivity, and finite affine constitutive
separability, the transfer algebra also covers conduction, diffusion,
dielectric, Darcy, and elasticity cell problems, although the numerical
assessment is restricted to three-dimensional linear elasticity in
short-fiber composites.


The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:homogenization} develops the general periodic cell problem,
its discrete Schur representation, and the geometry--material affine
separation. Section~\ref{sec:transfer} introduces the Sobol--Ritz transfer
operator, its variational properties, and the adaptive monitoring and
held-out validation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:numerical_examples}
specializes the formulation to three-dimensional elasticity and presents the
numerical assessment. Supporting derivations, constitutive details,
computational scaling, and reproducibility information are provided in the
appendices.
\section{General parametric periodic homogenization}
\label{sec:homogenization}

\subsection{Periodic cell problem and effective operator}
\label{subsec:cell_problem}

Let \(\Omega\) be a periodic representative cell with fixed microstructure
\(\G\), and let \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Xi\) denote the queried constitutive
parameters, where \(\Xi\) is the admissible constitutive-parameter domain.

The macroscopic loading is represented by \(g\in\R^m\), where \(m\) is the
number of independent loading directions and \(g_i\) is the amplitude of the
\(i\)-th unit loading state \(\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i\). An arbitrary
macroscopic loading is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:macro_field}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
g_i\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i.
\end{equation}

The field in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field} represents the macroscopic loading
applied to the periodic cell. In a heterogeneous microstructure, the local
field is generally nonuniform because the constituent phases have different
properties. For example, in linear elasticity, an imposed macroscopic strain
is redistributed differently between the matrix and fibers.

Let \(\boldsymbol x\in\Omega\) denote the spatial position within the cell.
The microscopic redistribution is described by a periodic fluctuation
\(u(\boldsymbol x)\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}\), whose mean is fixed to zero:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zero_mean}
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\int_{\Omega}
u(\boldsymbol x)\,
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x
=
0.
\end{equation}
Here, \(|\Omega|\) is the cell volume. Periodicity ensures compatibility
between opposite faces and that the fluctuation does not alter the imposed
macroscopic average.

The local field is written as the macroscopic field plus a microscopic
correction,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_field}
\boldsymbol z(\boldsymbol x;g)
=
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u(\boldsymbol x),
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal L\) maps the fluctuation to the corresponding physical field.
For scalar transport, \(\mathcal L=\nabla\), whereas for linear elasticity,
\(\mathcal L=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\). In elasticity,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_field} becomes
\(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol x)
=\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(u)\), so the
local strain consists of the imposed macroscopic strain and the
microscopic correction induced by the heterogeneous microstructure.

Once the local field is defined, the material response is determined by the
symmetric positive-definite constitutive operator
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)\), where
\(\boldsymbol x\) identifies the local phase and orientation,
\(\boldsymbol\xi\) contains the queried constitutive properties, and
\(\G\) defines the fixed microstructure. In linear elasticity,
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb C\) and
\(\boldsymbol\sigma(\boldsymbol x)
=\mathbb C(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\boldsymbol x)\),
so the local stiffness maps the redistributed strain to the corresponding
stress.

For a prescribed macroscopic loading \(g\), the equilibrium fluctuation
\(u_g\) is determined from the weak cell problem
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:general_cell_problem}
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L v)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u_g
\right]
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x
=
0
\qquad
\forall v\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(v\) is an admissible periodic test function. In linear elasticity,
\(\mathcal L=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\) and
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb C\), so Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem} reduces to
\(\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(v)^T\boldsymbol{\sigma}
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x=0\), the weak form of mechanical equilibrium; in
the absence of body forces, this corresponds to
\(\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0\).

Once \(u_g\) is obtained, the heterogeneous cell is represented by an
effective macroscopic operator \(\Heff\) that preserves its minimum energy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:effective_energy_definition}
\frac{1}{2}
g^T\Heff(\boldsymbol{\xi};\G)g
=
\min_{u\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}}
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u
\right]^T
\mathbb A
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u
\right]
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x.
\end{equation}
The minimizer is the equilibrium fluctuation \(u_g\); hence, \(\Heff\)
defines the homogeneous operator with the same macroscopic energy as the
resolved heterogeneous cell.

In linear elasticity, \(\Heff=C^{\mathrm{eff}}\). The corresponding
quantities for the other linear problems considered here are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:general_physics}. For transport laws conventionally written
with a minus sign, such as Fourier, Fick, and Darcy laws, the sign is absorbed
into the definition of the macroscopic driving so that \(\Heff\) remains
positive definite.

\subsection{Discrete energy and effective Schur map}
\label{subsec:schur_map}

We now discretize Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} to obtain a
finite-dimensional algebraic problem. The fluctuation field
\(u(\boldsymbol x)\) is represented by \(u\in\R^n\), where \(n\) is the
number of microscopic degrees of freedom. The resulting quadratic energy is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:discrete_energy}
\mathcal E(u,g;\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\frac{1}{2}u^T K(\boldsymbol\xi)u
-
u^T B(\boldsymbol\xi)g
+
\frac{1}{2}g^T D(\boldsymbol\xi)g.
\end{equation}
Here, \(K\in\R^{n\times n}\) governs the microscopic fluctuation,
\(B\in\R^{n\times m}\) couples the microscopic and macroscopic fields, and
\(D\in\R^{m\times m}\) represents the direct macroscopic energy contribution.
The derivation from Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}.

Minimization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} with respect to \(u\) gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stationarity}
K(\boldsymbol\xi)u
=
B(\boldsymbol\xi)g,
\end{equation}
where \(B\) follows the sign convention adopted in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy}.

The responses to the \(m\) independent unit macroscopic loading states are
collected in \(X=[x_1,\ldots,x_m]\in\R^{n\times m}\). They satisfy the
full-order response problem
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fom_response}
\boxed{
K(\boldsymbol\xi)X(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
B(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
Each column of \(X\) is the microscopic fluctuation associated with one unit
loading state. Consequently, \(X=K^{-1}B\), and the response to an arbitrary
loading vector \(g\) is \(u=Xg\).

Substituting the stationary response \(u=Xg\), with \(KX=B\), into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} eliminates the microscopic degrees of freedom
and yields the effective Schur map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schur_transfer}
\boxed{
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B(\boldsymbol\xi)^T X(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B(\boldsymbol\xi)^T
K(\boldsymbol\xi)^{-1}
B(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
The complete derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} from the continuous
effective-energy formulation is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}.

This Schur map condenses the \(n\)-dimensional microscopic problem into the
\(m\times m\) effective operator required by homogenization. In
three-dimensional linear elasticity, \(m=6\).

\subsection{Geometry--material affine separation}
\label{subsec:affine_separation}

The effective operator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} depends on the
discrete blocks \(K\), \(B\), and \(D\), which vary with the queried
constitutive parameters \(\boldsymbol\xi\). To separate this material
dependence from the fixed microstructure \(\G\), the local constitutive
operator is assumed to admit the finite affine representation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_affine}
\boxed{
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G).
}
\end{equation}
Here, \(Q\) is the number of affine terms,
\(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) contains the constitutive dependence, while
\(\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)\) contains the fixed spatial information of
the microstructure, including phase distribution and local orientations.

In linear elasticity, the coefficients \(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) are
obtained from the material properties of the matrix and fibers, whereas the
fields \(\mathbb A_q\) describe their spatial distribution and orientation.
For the constitutive family considered in this work, this separation is
exact; its elasticity-specific construction is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:affine}.

Because the discrete operators \(K\), \(B\), and \(D\) depend linearly on
\(\mathbb A\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} leads to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:affine_operators}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
K(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_q(\G),\\
B(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_q(\G),\\
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)D_q(\G).
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}
The derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:affine_discrete_derivation}.

For a fixed microstructure, the geometry-dependent blocks are computed once
and collected in
\(\mathcal K_{\G}=\{K_q(\G),B_q(\G),D_q(\G)\}_{q=1}^{Q}\).
A new material query changes only
\(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi)
=[\gamma_1(\boldsymbol\xi),\ldots,\gamma_Q(\boldsymbol\xi)]^T\).
Thus, the microstructure is compiled once, while each new material enters
only through its constitutive coefficients. A change in \(\G\), however,
requires a new offline compilation.
\section{Sobol--Ritz transfer operator}
\label{sec:transfer}

The affine separation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} separates the
constitutive parameters from the fixed microstructure, but a direct
evaluation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} would still require a
full-dimensional microscopic solve for every new material. The purpose of
the present section is to replace these repeated solves by a small Ritz
problem constructed once for each fixed microstructure.

The construction proceeds in two stages. Offline, full-order responses are
used to construct a common microscopic response space, determine its required
size through independent monitoring, and compile the geometry-dependent
affine operators in reduced coordinates. Online, a new material changes only
the affine coefficients \(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\), so its effective
response is obtained from small dense matrices without a new
full-dimensional solve.


\subsection{Offline construction of the response space}
\label{subsec:reduced_space}

Let
\(\Xi_C=\{\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(N_C)}\}\subset\Xi\)
be a fixed Sobol candidate sequence traversed in its prescribed order. Its
first \(N_s\) points define the final training prefix
\(\Xi_S=\{\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(N_s)}\}\), where the prefix length \(N_s\) is not
prescribed beforehand but is selected by the adaptive monitoring procedure
introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:heldout_validation}.

For every training material \(\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(j)}\), the full-order
problem in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} is solved for the \(m\) independent
macroscopic loading states. Equation~\eqref{eq:snapshot_matrix} collects
these microscopic responses into a common snapshot matrix:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snapshot_matrix}
S
=
\left[
X(\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(1)}),\ldots,
X(\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(N_s)})
\right]
\in\R^{n\times p},
\qquad
p=mN_s.
\end{equation}
Thus, each training material contributes \(m\) full microscopic response
fields, and the columns of \(S\) describe the response patterns observed
over the sampled material domain.

The dimension of the space generated by these responses is given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:full_snapshot_rank}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:full_snapshot_rank}
r
=
\operatorname{rank}(S)
\leq p.
\end{equation}
The complete snapshot span is retained throughout the construction. In the
reported campaign, all snapshot columns are linearly independent, so
\(r=p\). Despite retaining the complete snapshot span, its dimension remains
much smaller than that of the full microscopic problem, that is,
\(r\ll n\).

Having defined the snapshot space in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:snapshot_matrix}--\eqref{eq:full_snapshot_rank}, the next step
is to restrict the geometry-dependent blocks \(K_q\) and \(B_q\), introduced
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}, to this space. Before projection, these
blocks have dimensions
\(K_q\in\R^{n\times n}\) and
\(B_q\in\R^{n\times m}\), whereas
\(S\in\R^{n\times p}\), with \(p\ll n\).

Let \(s_i\) denote the \(i\)-th column of \(S\). A microscopic response
restricted to the snapshot space is written as
\(u_r=Sa=\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_i s_i\), where
\(a=[a_1,\ldots,a_p]^T\in\R^p\) contains the snapshot-coordinate weights.
Thus, instead of solving directly for the \(n\) microscopic unknowns in
\(u_r\), the projected problem determines only the \(p\) coefficients in
\(a\).

To obtain equations for these coefficients, consider the full microscopic
equilibrium relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity}. Substituting \(u_r=Sa\)
gives the residual
\(B(\boldsymbol\xi)g-K(\boldsymbol\xi)Sa\). Galerkin projection requires this
residual to be orthogonal to the snapshot space, giving
\(S^T[B(\boldsymbol\xi)g-K(\boldsymbol\xi)Sa]=0\), or equivalently
\(S^TK(\boldsymbol\xi)Sa=S^TB(\boldsymbol\xi)g\).
Using the affine representations in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} then
gives
\(\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)(S^TK_qS)a
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)(S^TB_q)g\).
Hence, each geometry-dependent affine contribution can be projected and
stored independently of the queried material. The blocks
\(D_q\in\R^{m\times m}\) require no analogous projection because they
already act entirely on the macroscopic loading space. The resulting raw
snapshot-coordinate blocks are defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_affine_blocks}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:raw_affine_blocks}
\widetilde K_q
=
S^T K_q S
\in\R^{p\times p},
\qquad
\widetilde B_q
=
S^T B_q
\in\R^{p\times m},
\qquad
q=1,\ldots,Q.
\end{equation}

The voxel contractions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_affine_blocks} use the symmetry
and small constitutive rank of each local affine basis. For orientation group
\(g\), its exact signed spectral representation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:constitutive_factor_ritz}
\mathbb A_{q,g}
=
U_{q,g}\Lambda_qU_{q,g}^T,
\qquad
z^T\mathbb A_{q,g}y
=
(U_{q,g}^Tz)^T\Lambda_q(U_{q,g}^Ty).
\end{equation}
Here, \(\Lambda_q\) retains the signs of all nonzero eigenvalues of the
symmetric \(6\times6\) constitutive basis; only arithmetic-zero
eigendirections are omitted. Local rotations change \(U_{q,g}\) but preserve
\(\Lambda_q\). Equation~\eqref{eq:constitutive_factor_ritz} therefore changes
the contraction kernel and its data movement without approximating the
constitutive action or altering the retained snapshot span.

Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_affine_blocks} applies to every intermediate snapshot
space and is therefore used throughout the adaptive construction. At
enrichment step \(\ell\), let \(S_\ell\) denote the snapshot matrix generated
by the first \(\ell\) Sobol materials, with
\(p_\ell=m\ell\). As new microscopic responses are appended, the
corresponding raw snapshot-coordinate blocks become
\(\widetilde K_q^{(\ell)}=S_\ell^T K_qS_\ell\) and
\(\widetilde B_q^{(\ell)}=S_\ell^TB_q\).
These intermediate blocks provide the reduced systems used by the adaptive
monitoring procedure described next.


\subsection{Adaptive training, monitoring, and held-out validation}
\label{subsec:heldout_validation}

The construction in Section~\ref{subsec:reduced_space} defines a snapshot
space and its raw projected operators for every prefix of the fixed Sobol
candidate sequence \(\Xi_C\). It remains to determine how many of these
training materials are required. This selection is made using an independent
monitor set
\(\Xi_M=\{\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(N_M)}\}\).

Each monitor material is solved once with the full-order model to obtain its
full-order effective operator \(\Hfom\). Its microscopic response fields are
used only for error evaluation: they are never appended to \(S_\ell\) and
therefore never modify the Ritz response space.

For the \(k\)-th monitor material
\(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)}\) at enrichment step \(\ell\), the raw blocks from
Section~\ref{subsec:reduced_space} are combined with its affine coefficients
to assemble
\(\widetilde K_\ell(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
\widetilde K_q^{(\ell)}\) and
\(\widetilde B_\ell(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
\widetilde B_q^{(\ell)}\).
The corresponding snapshot-coordinate matrix \(A_\ell^{(k)}\) is obtained
from
\(\widetilde K_\ell(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})A_\ell^{(k)}
=\widetilde B_\ell(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})\).
Since the microscopic Ritz approximation is
\(X_\ell^{(k)}=S_\ell A_\ell^{(k)}\), the Schur form in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} gives
\(H_\ell^{\mathrm{eff}}(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
=D(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
-\widetilde B_\ell(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})^TA_\ell^{(k)}\).
Thus, monitor materials can be evaluated entirely in the current raw
snapshot coordinates while the Sobol prefix is being enlarged.

To determine whether the current snapshot space is sufficiently accurate,
the largest relative effective-operator error over the monitor set is used.
This defines the monitor error in Eq.~\eqref{eq:monitor_stopping}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:monitor_stopping}
e_M^{(\ell)}
=
\max_{k=1,\ldots,N_M}
\frac{
\left\|
H_\ell^{\mathrm{eff}}(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
-
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
\right\|_F
}{
\left\|
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(k)})
\right\|_F
}.
\end{equation}
Thus, \(e_M^{(\ell)}\) is the worst effective-operator error observed by the
monitor set for the current Sobol prefix.

Monitoring begins when the prefix reaches the prescribed minimum size
\(\ell_{\min}\). Thereafter, one Sobol material is appended at each
enrichment step until the monitor error first satisfies the prescribed
tolerance \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ROM}}\). The resulting first-crossing rule
defines the final training size in Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive_stop}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:adaptive_stop}
N_s
=
\min
\left\{
\ell\in\{\ell_{\min},\ldots,N_C\}:
e_M^{(\ell)}
\leq
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ROM}}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
If no candidate prefix satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive_stop}, the prescribed
tolerance is reported as unmet.

The monitor set therefore controls only the stopping point of the prescribed
Sobol sequence. It neither changes the order of \(\Xi_C\) nor selects
alternative training materials, and its microscopic response fields never
enter the snapshot space. Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive_stop} is evaluated
only over the finite set \(\Xi_M\), it is a practical adaptive stopping
criterion rather than a uniform error bound over the complete constitutive
domain \(\Xi\).

Once Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive_stop} determines \(N_s\), the final snapshot
matrix \(S=S_{N_s}\) is frozen and no further response directions are added.
The approximation space is therefore fixed; the remaining offline step is to
replace its raw coordinates by a physically scaled coordinate system. This
is useful because the snapshot directions may have different energy scales
and strong correlations, which can produce poorly scaled reduced operators.
A reference operator is therefore introduced to define a common
reference-energy metric for the frozen snapshot space.

The reference operator is constructed from the complete Sobol candidate
sequence. Only its affine coefficient vectors are averaged, so no additional
full-order solutions are required. The resulting reference operator is
defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reference_energy_operator}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reference_energy_operator}
\overline{\boldsymbol\gamma}
=
\frac{1}{N_C}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_C}
\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(j)}),
\qquad
K_{\mathrm{ref}}
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\overline{\gamma}_qK_q.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators},
\(K_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is equivalently
\(N_C^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_C}K(\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(j)})\).
It therefore provides a single reference operator representative of the
prescribed constitutive domain rather than of one particular training
material. Candidate points outside the selected prefix contribute only their
coefficient vectors
\(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(j)})\); they require no microscopic
solve and contribute no snapshots.

The frozen snapshot space is normalized with respect to this operator through
the reference-energy Gram matrix
\(G_{\mathrm{ref}}=S^TK_{\mathrm{ref}}S\).
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:reference_energy_operator} and using the raw
contractions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_affine_blocks} gives
\(G_{\mathrm{ref}}
=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\overline{\gamma}_q\widetilde K_q\).
Hence, the reference-energy Gram matrix is assembled entirely from the
already available small snapshot-coordinate blocks and requires no
additional contraction over the full voxel grid.

In the reported construction, all retained snapshot columns are linearly
independent, so \(r=p\). Together with the coercivity of
\(K_{\mathrm{ref}}\), this makes \(G_{\mathrm{ref}}\) positive definite and
permits the reference-energy Cholesky--QR normalization in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reference_energy_qr}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reference_energy_qr}
G_{\mathrm{ref}}
=
S^TK_{\mathrm{ref}}S
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\overline{\gamma}_q\widetilde K_q
=
R_{\mathrm{ref}}^TR_{\mathrm{ref}},
\qquad
V_r
=
SR_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-1}.
\end{equation}

Here, \(R_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is the upper Cholesky factor of
\(G_{\mathrm{ref}}\), and \(V_r\) is the corresponding reference-energy Ritz
basis. Since \(R_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is invertible,
\(\operatorname{range}(V_r)=\operatorname{range}(S)\), so the transformation
changes only the coordinates and preserves the complete snapshot space.
Moreover, Eq.~\eqref{eq:reference_energy_qr} gives
\(V_r^TK_{\mathrm{ref}}V_r=I\).
Thus, the retained response directions share a common reference-energy scale
and are mutually orthogonal under this metric without changing the Ritz
approximation space.

The geometry-dependent affine blocks are then transformed into their final
reduced coordinates. Starting from
\(K_{q,r}=V_r^TK_qV_r\) and \(B_{q,r}=V_r^TB_q\), substituting
\(V_r=SR_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-1}\) and using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_affine_blocks} gives the final reduced blocks in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:raw_ritz_transform}
K_{q,r}
=
R_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-T}
\widetilde K_q
R_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-1}
\in\R^{r\times r},
\qquad
B_{q,r}
=
R_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-T}
\widetilde B_q
\in\R^{r\times m}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform} applies the final coordinate
transformation directly to the already contracted blocks. Consequently, the
full-dimensional basis \(V_r\) need not be formed explicitly and no
additional full-dimensional contraction is required. Together with
\(D_q\in\R^{m\times m}\), the set
\(\{K_{q,r},B_{q,r},D_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}\) constitutes the frozen
geometry-dependent representation.

The accuracy of this frozen model is finally assessed with a second,
independent held-out validation set
\(\Xi_V=\{\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(N_V)}\}\). These materials have no role in training,
monitoring, stopping, or reference-energy normalization. For the \(h\)-th
held-out material, the relative effective-operator error is defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:validation_error}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:validation_error}
e_{V,h}
=
\frac{
\left\|
\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(h)})
-
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(h)})
\right\|_F
}{
\left\|
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(h)})
\right\|_F
},
\qquad
h=1,\ldots,N_V.
\end{equation}
The full-order responses required by Eq.~\eqref{eq:validation_error} are
evaluated only after the reduced representation has been frozen. They
therefore cannot influence \(N_s\), the snapshot space, or the compiled
geometry-dependent blocks.

The monitor and held-out validation sets consequently have distinct roles:
\(\Xi_M\) determines when enough microscopic response information has been
collected to stop the sequential Sobol enrichment, whereas \(\Xi_V\) measures
the accuracy of the final frozen model. This separation keeps model
construction and final performance assessment independent.


\subsection{Online Ritz--Schur transfer}
\label{subsec:reduced_transfer}

The preceding offline construction produces the frozen geometry-dependent
representation
\(\{K_{q,r},B_{q,r},D_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}\). The purpose of the online stage is to
map a new constitutive point \(\boldsymbol\xi\) to its effective operator
using only this reduced representation. The procedure consists of three
steps: the material-dependent reduced operators are first assembled from its
affine coefficients, a small Ritz system is then solved for the microscopic
response coordinates, and the effective operator is finally recovered through
the Schur map.

A queried material enters this procedure only through
\(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\). From the Galerkin definitions
\(K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)=V_r^TK(\boldsymbol\xi)V_r\) and
\(B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)=V_r^TB(\boldsymbol\xi)\), substitution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} together with the precompiled blocks in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform} gives the material-dependent reduced
operators. Since \(D_q\) already acts on the macroscopic loading space, its
contributions are combined directly. The complete online assembly is
therefore given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:online_assembly}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_{q,r},
\\
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_{q,r},
\\
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)D_q.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}
Thus, changing the constituent properties only changes the coefficients used
to combine the frozen blocks; no new full-dimensional projection or
geometry-dependent operator construction is required.

Once the reduced operators for the queried material have been assembled, its
microscopic response is sought in the fixed Ritz space as
\(X_r(\boldsymbol\xi)=V_r\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi)\), where
\(\widehat X_r\in\R^{r\times m}\) contains the response coordinates in the
reference-energy basis \(V_r\). Substituting this representation into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} and imposing Galerkin orthogonality gives the
reduced equilibrium problem in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced_response}
\boxed{
K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
The \(n\)-dimensional microscopic system is therefore replaced online by a
dense system of dimension \(r\), with \(r\ll n\).

With the reduced microscopic response available, the final step is to recover
the effective operator through the same Schur structure as the full-order
model. Substituting
\(X_r=V_r\widehat X_r\) into \(D-B^TX_r\) and using
\(B_r=V_r^TB\) gives
\(B^TX_r=B_r^T\widehat X_r\). The online Ritz--Schur transfer is therefore
given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced_transfer}
\boxed{
\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)^T
\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
Consequently, a new material is mapped from its affine constitutive
coefficients directly to its effective operator without solving the original
microscopic problem or reconstructing the full-dimensional response field.

For fixed \(m\), assembling the affine reduced operators requires
\(\mathcal O(Qr^2)\) work, while the dense reduced solve has leading cost
\(\mathcal O(r^3)\). The overall online complexity is therefore
\(\mathcal O(Qr^2+r^3)\). By comparison, one full-order
Fourier--Galerkin material evaluation scales as
\(\mathcal O(mI_{\mathrm{CG}}N_{\mathrm{vox}}
\log N_{\mathrm{vox}})\).
The online model therefore depends on the small response-space dimension
\(r\), whereas the full-order evaluation remains tied to the voxel resolution
of the microstructure.

Beyond reducing the online dimension, the Ritz construction preserves the
variational structure of the full-order problem. For a fixed material, let
\(E_X=X-X_r\) denote the microscopic response error and
\(R=B-KX_r\) the corresponding residual. The resulting Ritz--Schur identity
is stated in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_schur_identity}
\boxed{
\Hr-\Hfom
=
E_X^TKE_X
=
R^TK^{-1}R
\succeq0.
}
\end{equation}
Thus, the effective-operator difference is exactly the microscopic Ritz error
measured in the energy induced by \(K\), and \(\Hr\) is an upper
approximation of \(\Hfom\) in the Loewner ordering. The complete derivation,
including the residual--error relation and Galerkin cancellation of the cross
term, is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:ritz_schur}.

The same variational structure provides an ordering between nested Ritz
spaces. For two trial spaces satisfying
\(\operatorname{range}(V_r)\subseteq\operatorname{range}(V_s)\), minimization
of the same discrete energy over the nested spaces gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}
\boxed{
\Hfom
\preceq
\Hs
\preceq
\Hr.
}
\end{equation}
Hence, enlarging the Ritz trial space cannot worsen the effective-operator
approximation in exact arithmetic. A complete variational proof is provided
in Appendix~\ref{app:ritz_schur}.

This result applies directly to the sequential Sobol enrichment introduced
in Section~\ref{subsec:reduced_space}. Because each enrichment step appends
new response columns without removing previous ones, the snapshot spaces
satisfy
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_nested_spaces}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fullrank_nested_spaces}
\operatorname{range}(S_\ell)
\subseteq
\operatorname{range}(S_{\ell+1}).
\end{equation}
Combining this nesting with Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering} gives the
monotone enrichment ordering in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur}
\boxed{
\Hfom
\preceq
H_{\ell+1}^{\mathrm{eff}}
\preceq
H_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}.
}
\end{equation}
Therefore, each Sobol enrichment enlarges the available response space and
moves the corresponding full-span Ritz effective operator monotonically
toward the full-order operator in exact arithmetic. The detailed proof is
given in Appendix~\ref{app:ritz_schur}.


\subsection{Offline--online workflow}
\label{subsec:offline_online}

The complete procedure is summarized in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:offline_online}. For each fixed microstructure \(\G\),
the offline construction comprises the geometry-dependent affine blocks,
full-order training and monitor solves, sequential snapshot enrichment,
raw snapshot-coordinate contractions, adaptive stopping, and the final
reference-energy Cholesky--QR normalization. Once the reduced representation
has been frozen, the held-out validation is performed only as an independent
post-freeze assessment and cannot modify the model. A subsequent online
material query then requires only evaluation of
\(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\), assembly of the reduced operators
through Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly}, solution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response}, and evaluation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer}. No full-order solve or full-dimensional
response reconstruction is required online.

using Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform}. \\

Freeze
\(\{K_{q,r},B_{q,r},D_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}\)
and record the reduced-model hash.

\end{tabbing}


\textbf{Post-freeze validation}

\begin{tabbing}
\hspace{0.5cm}\=\hspace{0.7cm}\=\kill

Generate the independent held-out validation set \(\Xi_V\). \\

\textbf{for} \(h=1,\ldots,N_V\) \textbf{do} \\

\> Evaluate
\(\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(h)})\)
with the FOM. \\

\> Evaluate
\(\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(h)})\)
with the frozen reduced model. \\

\> Compute \(e_{V,h}\) using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:validation_error}. \\

\> \textbf{if} the reduced evaluation fails \textbf{then} \\

\>\> Record a numerical failure without regularizing, rebuilding, or
modifying the frozen operators. \\

\> \textbf{end if} \\

\textbf{end for}

\end{tabbing}


\textbf{Online query}

\begin{tabbing}
\hspace{0.5cm}\=\hspace{0.7cm}\=\kill

\textbf{Given}
a new material point \(\boldsymbol\xi^\star\): \\

Evaluate
\(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\). \\

Assemble
\(K_r(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\),
\(B_r(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\), and
\(D(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\)
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly}. \\

Solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response} for
\(\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\). \\

Evaluate
\(\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\)
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer}. \\

\textbf{return}
\(\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\).

\end{tabbing}

\end{algorithm}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Reduced-basis homogenization, affine offline--online decomposition, Schur
condensation, and variational preservation are established ingredients. The
methodological contribution of this work is their FFT-specific combination
into a constitutive compiler for repeated material queries on one fixed,
fully resolved periodic geometry. The construction collects all macroscopic
loading problems in an effective Schur map, separates material coefficients
from reusable voxel geometry blocks, and projects those blocks onto the
complete Sobol snapshot span. In exact arithmetic, this particular
Ritz--Schur construction makes the ROM--FOM operator difference positive
semidefinite and exactly equal to the \(K\)-energy of the microscopic response
error. Because enrichment appends snapshots without truncation, the trial
spaces are nested and the effective operators are ordered monotonically
toward the full-order operator. The implementation forms the raw affine
blocks with exact signed spectral factors, gathers orientation-dependent
factors once per voxel chunk, overlaps pinned transfers with GPU computation,
and applies one reference-energy Cholesky--QR transformation at freeze. The
mixed-precision realization is assessed numerically and is not treated as a
bitwise certificate of the exact identity.

The formulation is not restricted to elasticity. It applies directly to
linear, symmetric, and coercive periodic cell problems such as thermal and
electrical conduction, mass diffusion, dielectric electrostatics, linear
Darcy transport, and linear elasticity whenever the local constitutive law
admits the required finite separable representation. The number of
macroscopic loadings changes with the physics, but the transfer algebra
remains unchanged.

Geometry is handled implicitly through the full-order operators and snapshots
used by the full-span Ritz construction. Consequently, no
morphology-specific reduced descriptor is required. The framework is
independent of the geometry class, while each compiled operator remains
specific to the fixed geometry from which it was constructed.

The ten three-dimensional elasticity examples selected Sobol prefixes between
6 and 16 materials and full snapshot ranks between 36 and 96. On the
independent 20-point affine-maximin design, 89 of 200 material--geometry pairs
satisfied the \(10^{-4}\) relative Frobenius target and 195 satisfied
\(10^{-3}\); the mean and maximum errors were \(2.138\times10^{-4}\) and
\(1.306\times10^{-3}\). Thus, stopping at the
first crossing over five monitors does not certify the same tolerance on a
wider space-filling design. No held-out point was used for subsequent
enrichment, and every reduced system and stiffness tensor remained positive
definite. The raw Schur diagnostic is retained as a secondary record of the
finite-precision limit of the exact variational ordering.

At the same geometry-wise Sobol prefixes, the physics-based transfer model
outperformed direct RBF and Kriging surrogates in all 200 paired comparisons.
The training-only-tuned baseline mean errors, \(6.293\times10^{-2}\) and
\(7.258\times10^{-2}\), were approximately 294 and 339 times the
Sobol--Ritz mean error. This comparison isolates the number of full-order
training materials while recognizing that the transfer construction retains
six microscopic fields per material and the data-only surrogates retain only
the effective tensor.

Across G00--G09, median FOM material times ranged from
\(0.127\) to \(7.019~\si{\second}\), whereas synchronized CuPy/CUDA batches
required 2.42--8.28~\si{\micro\second} per material after amortization. The
corresponding throughput speed-ups were \(2.33\times10^4\) to
\(1.14\times10^6\). After including all five monitor-reference solves
independently of cache reuse, the compilation costs were recovered after
12--27 material queries.

The resolution study further showed that voxel refinement is most important
for the dense long-fiber geometry: its tensor error relative to the
result at 6 voxels per fiber diameter decreased from \(5.38\%\) at resolution
3 to \(0.91\%\) at resolution 5. This discretization effect is distinct from
ROM error and should be considered when the compiled operator is used as a
high-accuracy surrogate of the underlying microstructure.

The three-dimensional elasticity specialization illustrates how the abstract
operators become stiffness tensors, Mandel rotations, and seven affine
constitutive coefficients. Extensions to the scalar
transport problems listed in Table~\ref{tab:general_physics} require only the
corresponding local constitutive operator, macroscopic loading basis, affine
coefficient map, and validation protocol. Mixed pore-scale flow and
nonlinear problems require additional stability or nonlinear reduction
machinery and remain outside the direct scope of the present formulation.

\section{Derivation of the discrete Schur representation}
\label{app:schur_derivation}

Starting from the effective-energy definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition}, expansion of the quadratic
integrand separates the microscopic, coupling, and macroscopic contributions,
\[
\mathcal E
=
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L u)^T\mathbb A(\mathcal L u)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x
+
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L u)^T\mathbb A\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x
+
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)^T
\mathbb A
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x.
\]

After spatial discretization, write
\(u(\boldsymbol x)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}u_a\phi_a(\boldsymbol x)\).
Using the macroscopic loading expansion in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field}, the
three discrete blocks are
\begin{align}
K_{ab}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\\
B_{ai}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
-
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\\
D_{ij}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\int_{\Omega}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_j
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\end{align}
with \(a,b=1,\ldots,n\) and \(i,j=1,\ldots,m\). These definitions give the
discrete energy in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy}; the minus sign in \(B\)
accounts for the cross-energy convention used there.

Stationarity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} with respect to \(u\) gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity}. Collecting the \(m\) unit-load responses then
gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response}, so \(X=K^{-1}B\) and \(u=Xg\).
Substitution into the discrete energy yields
\[
\mathcal E(Xg,g)
=
\frac{1}{2}g^TX^TKXg
-
g^TX^TBg
+
\frac{1}{2}g^TDg.
\]
Using \(KX=B\) and \(K=K^T\) gives
\(X^TKX=X^TB=B^TX\), and therefore
\[
\mathcal E(Xg,g)
=
\frac{1}{2}
g^T
\left[
D-B^TX
\right]
g.
\]

\section{Derivation of the affine discrete operators}
\label{app:affine_discrete_derivation}

The discrete affine operators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} follow from
the local separation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} and the block definitions
in Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}. For example, substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} into the definition of \(K_{ab}\) gives
\[
K_{ab}(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x.
\]
Because \(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) is independent of
\(\boldsymbol x\), the remaining integral defines
\[
(K_q)_{ab}
=
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\]
and hence
\(K(\boldsymbol\xi)=\sum_q\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_q(\G)\).

Applying the same substitution to the \(B\) and \(D\) definitions in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation} gives
\[
B(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_q(\G),
\qquad
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)D_q(\G),
\]
which completes the derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}.

\section{Ritz--Schur identity and variational ordering}
\label{app:ritz_schur}

This appendix proves the structure-preserving property stated in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity} and
\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}. Consider one fixed material point
\(\boldsymbol\xi\) and suppress its dependence for readability. Let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_error_residual}
E_X=X-X_r,
\qquad
R=B-KX_r.
\end{equation}
Using the full-order equation \(KX=B\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_error_relation}
R
=
KX-KX_r
=
K E_X.
\end{equation}
Since \(K\) is invertible on the constrained periodic space,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:error_from_residual}
E_X=K^{-1}R.
\end{equation}

From the full-order and reduced Schur maps,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} and \eqref{eq:reduced_transfer},
\begin{align}
\Hr-\Hfom
&=
\left(D-B^TX_r\right)
-
\left(D-B^TX\right)
\nonumber\\
&=
B^T(X-X_r)
=
B^TE_X.
\label{eq:schur_difference_first}
\end{align}
Because \(B=KX=K(X_r+E_X)\) and \(K=K^T\),
\begin{align}
B^TE_X
&=
(X_r+E_X)^T K E_X
\nonumber\\
&=
X_r^T K E_X
+
E_X^T K E_X.
\label{eq:schur_difference_expand}
\end{align}

The Galerkin equation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:galerkin_residual_orthogonality}
V_r^T(KX_r-B)=0,
\end{equation}
or, with the residual definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_error_residual},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:galerkin_residual_zero}
V_r^TR=0.
\end{equation}
Using \(X_r=V_r\widehat X_r\) and
\(K E_X=R\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual_error_relation},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cross_term_zero}
X_r^T K E_X
=
\widehat X_r^T V_r^T R
=
0.
\end{equation}
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_difference_expand} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_energy_identity_appendix}
\Hr-\Hfom
=
E_X^T K E_X.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_from_residual} and the symmetry of \(K\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_energy_identity_appendix}
E_X^T K E_X
=
R^T K^{-1}R.
\end{equation}
Finally, for any \(g\in\R^m\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_psd_proof}
g^T(\Hr-\Hfom)g
=
(E_Xg)^T K(E_Xg)
\geq 0,
\end{equation}
because \(K\) is positive definite. Therefore,
\[
\Hr-\Hfom\succeq0,
\]
which proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity}.

The nested-space ordering follows directly from the same variational
structure. For a fixed macroscopic loading \(g\), the full-order solution
minimizes the discrete energy over the complete admissible microscopic space,
whereas the Ritz solutions minimize the same energy over their respective
trial spaces. Thus,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:restricted_energy_minima}
\frac{1}{2}g^T\Hfom g
=
\min_{u\in\R^n}\mathcal E(u,g),
\qquad
\frac{1}{2}g^T\Hr g
=
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_r)}\mathcal E(u,g),
\end{equation}
with the understood periodic constraint already incorporated in the discrete
space. If
\(\operatorname{span}(V_r)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(V_s)\), then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nested_minima}
\min_{u\in\R^n}\mathcal E(u,g)
\leq
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_s)}\mathcal E(u,g)
\leq
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_r)}\mathcal E(u,g).
\end{equation}
Hence, for every \(g\in\R^m\),
\[
g^T\Hfom g
\leq
g^T\Hs g
\leq
g^T\Hr g,
\]
which is equivalent to the Loewner ordering
\[
\Hfom
\preceq
\Hs
\preceq
\Hr.
\]
This proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}. In the present full-span
construction, the trial spaces are precisely
\(\operatorname{range}(S_\ell)\). Appending a new response block preserves
every previous column, so
\(\operatorname{range}(S_\ell)\subseteq
\operatorname{range}(S_{\ell+1})\) without an additional assumption. Taking
\(V_r\) and \(V_s\) as any orthonormal coordinates for these two spans gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur}. Moreover, if
\(A=H_{\ell+1}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\Hfom\) and
\(B=H_\ell^{\mathrm{eff}}-\Hfom\), then
\(0\preceq A\preceq B\). Monotonicity of unitarily invariant norms on the
positive-semidefinite cone gives \(\|A\|_F\leq\|B\|_F\), establishing the
exact nonincrease of each monitor error under enrichment.

\section{Computational scaling and numerical implementation}
\label{app:computational_details}

This appendix collects the implementation-level costs omitted from the main
methodological development. The purpose is to separate the asymptotic cost of
the Fourier--Galerkin full-order model from the offline contractions required
to compile the ROM and from the final online query.

Let
\[
N_{\mathrm{vox}}=N_xN_yN_z
\]
be the total number of voxels in the periodic grid, let
\(p_\ell=m\ell\) be the number of raw snapshot columns after \(\ell\)
training materials, and let \(p=mN_s\) be the final raw snapshot dimension.
The full snapshot rank after enrichment \(\ell\) is denoted by
\(r_\ell\leq p_\ell\), with final rank \(r=r_{N_s}\).

For the Fourier--Galerkin/CG solver, one application of the discrete operator
requires a fixed number of FFTs. Since each FFT scales as
\(\mathcal O(N_{\mathrm{vox}}\log N_{\mathrm{vox}})\), the leading cost of
one material with all \(m\) independent loading cases is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{FOM}}
=
\mathcal O\!\left(
mI_{\mathrm{CG}}N_{\mathrm{vox}}\log N_{\mathrm{vox}}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(I_{\mathrm{CG}}\) denotes the representative CG iteration count. For
fixed physics, vector/tensor component counts contribute only constant
factors, and the FFT workspace scales as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{FOM}}
=
\mathcal O(mN_{\mathrm{vox}}).
\end{equation}

The raw snapshots are retained only until the reduced operators have been
frozen. Their storage therefore scales as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O(N_{\mathrm{vox}}p).
\end{equation}
Incremental exact affine Ritz contractions over the complete Sobol prefix
accumulate to
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O\!\left(QN_{\mathrm{vox}}p^2\right)
\end{equation}
work, with retained small-block storage
\(\mathcal O(Qp^2)\) in addition to the snapshots. The constitutive
factorization in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constitutive_factor_ritz} changes constants
and data movement but not this leading order. After adaptive stopping, the
reference-energy Cholesky factorization and all transformations in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform} are performed once, with the conservative
dense-algebra bound
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O\!\left((Q+1)p^3\right).
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the only cubic offline dependence is on the small raw snapshot
dimension \(p\), not on the voxel count. No eigendecomposition or dense
coordinate transformation is repeated during enrichment.

For one reduced material query, retaining the loading dimension explicitly,
\begin{align}
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{ROM}}(r,m,Q)
&=
\mathcal O\!\left[
Q(r^2+rm+m^2)+r^3+r^2m+rm^2
\right],
\label{eq:rom_time_complexity_appendix}\\
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{ROM}}(r,m)
&=
\mathcal O(r^2+rm+m^2).
\label{eq:rom_memory_complexity_appendix}
\end{align}
For fixed \(m\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:rom_time_complexity_appendix} reduces to the
leading dependence \(\mathcal O(Qr^2+r^3)\). The stored reduced model contains
the projected affine blocks and the small \(D_q\) contributions, requiring
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O\!\left[Q(r^2+rm+m^2)\right]
\end{equation}
memory.

The nominal campaign performs \(N_s+N_M+N_V\) high-fidelity material solves:
only the
\(N_s\) training materials contribute snapshots, the \(N_M\) monitor
materials control the stopping point, and the \(N_V\) validation materials
are evaluated with the FOM only after the reduced operators have been frozen.

The implementation bounds temporary memory without changing the resolved
snapshot span. Raw snapshot fields are stored in a preallocated contiguous
array at the configured storage precision and are ordered by phase and local
orientation. Each phase-supported voxel chunk uses the exact signed spectral
factors in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constitutive_factor_ritz}. For fiber chunks, the
orientation-dependent factors are gathered once per voxel and reused across
the five fiber coefficients. Old and new snapshot columns are likewise
uploaded once per chunk. The resulting small blocks remain resident and are
accumulated in \texttt{float64} on the GPU.

Pinned double buffering and separate transfer and compute streams overlap
CPU packing and host-to-device transfer of chunk \(c+1\) with the factorized
contractions of chunk \(c\). The pinned host allocation is capped at
1~GiB, the GPU chunk size is chosen from the affine workspace limit, and one
material is solved and appended at a time. Once the stopping prefix is known,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reference_energy_qr} and the triangular transforms are evaluated
once in \texttt{float64}; they require no additional voxel pass. No direction
is discarded in the reported campaign, for which \(r=p\) at every accepted
prefix. The nominal workflow does not probe, regularize, or rebuild a frozen
ROM. A failed reduced evaluation is recorded as a numerical failure.
The concrete precisions, tolerances, and memory limits used in the numerical
campaign are reported in Table~\ref{tab:campaign_protocol}.

After compilation and validation, full voxel fields are transient and are not
required by the deployed transfer model. Thus the online dependence is on
\(Q\), \(r\), and \(m\), whereas \(N_{\mathrm{vox}}\) appears only in the
offline Fourier--Galerkin and contraction stages. In particular, no
\(N_{\mathrm{vox}}^3\) operation is introduced by the method; the cubic
terms are confined to the reduced or snapshot coordinates \(r\) and \(p\).

\section{Training-only surrogate hyperparameter optimization}
\label{app:surrogate_hyperparameters}

The RBF and Kriging comparisons use the same Sobol prefix available to the
transfer model at each training size. Inputs are the seven affine
constitutive coefficients, normalized coordinatewise using ranges computed
from the fixed 1024-point candidate sequence. Outputs are the 21 independent
upper-triangular entries of the symmetric Mandel stiffness tensor and are
standardized using only the current training prefix.

For RBF, deterministic leave-one-out cross-validation minimizes the mean
relative Frobenius tensor error over eight kernel families: linear,
thin-plate, cubic, quintic, multiquadric, inverse-multiquadric,
inverse-quadratic, and Gaussian. The shape parameter spans 16 logarithmically
spaced multiples from \(2^{-12}\) to \(2^3\) of the inverse median pair
distance. Five smoothing levels from zero to \(10^{-1}\) and every polynomial
degree admissible at the current fold size are also considered.

For Kriging, the covariance family is selected by maximum optimized training
log marginal likelihood among ten isotropic and
automatic-relevance-determination variants of Mat\'ern,
squared-exponential, rational-quadratic, and Mat\'ern-plus-linear kernels.
Covariance amplitude, length scales, nugget, and the rational-quadratic shape
parameter when applicable are optimized continuously. Every candidate uses
two deterministic optimizer restarts during family selection; the winning
family is then refitted with ten deterministic restarts. This complete search
is repeated for every geometry and prefix. Monitor and held-out responses are
excluded from all hyperparameter decisions.
