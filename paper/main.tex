\begin{abstract}
Repeated computational homogenization becomes expensive when the same
microstructure must be solved for many different local constitutive
properties. This work formulates that setting as a reusable transfer problem
for a fixed periodic microstructure. The formulation applies to linear,
symmetric, and coercive periodic cell problems with separable constitutive
dependence, including thermal and electrical conduction, mass diffusion,
dielectric electrostatics, linear Darcy transport, and linear elasticity.
The independent macroscopic loading problems are assembled into one discrete
Schur map, while material dependence is separated from the fixed geometry
through an affine operator representation. During the offline stage, one
fixed Sobol sequence is traversed with the full-order Fourier--Galerkin model
and the resulting microscopic responses are appended to a common snapshot
matrix. Proper orthogonal decomposition is used only to orthonormalize the
complete snapshot span; no energy-retention threshold, numerical-rank
truncation, or prescribed reduced dimension is introduced. Because the raw
snapshot spaces are nested under enrichment, the resulting Ritz--Schur
operators converge monotonically in the Loewner sense in exact arithmetic.
Their difference from the full-order operator is positive semidefinite and
satisfies an exact microscopic energy identity.
Training follows a fixed Sobol prefix and is stopped adaptively using five
independent Fourier--Galerkin monitor materials. Starting from two training
materials, one material is appended at a time until the maximum monitor error
is at most \(10^{-4}\). The frozen model is then assessed once on a second,
independent 20-material maximin design selected from a 4096-point Sobol pool;
its FOM responses have no role in snapshot enrichment, span construction, or
stopping.
Across ten elastic microstructures, the adaptive construction selects between
6 and 16 training materials and full snapshot ranks between 36 and 96. The
wider held-out design exposes a generalization gap: 91 of 200
material--geometry pairs satisfy the \(10^{-4}\) target and 198 satisfy
\(10^{-3}\), with mean and maximum relative tensor errors of
\(2.134\times10^{-4}\) and \(1.297\times10^{-3}\), respectively.
All held-out reduced systems and stiffness tensors remain positive definite.
At the same geometry-wise training counts, the ROM has lower error than
training-only-tuned radial-basis-function and Kriging surrogates for all 200
paired comparisons; their aggregate mean errors are
\(6.256\times10^{-2}\) and \(7.585\times10^{-2}\), respectively.
The online stage uses only reduced dense systems assembled from the
precomputed affine blocks.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item A common transfer formulation covers linear coercive transport and elasticity cell problems.
\item Exact affine separation compiles fixed geometry while queried material enters only through constitutive coefficients.
\item Sobol sampling and full-rank POD retain the complete snapshot span without energy truncation.
\item Nested full-rank Ritz spaces give monotone Loewner convergence and an exact operator-error energy identity.
\item Same-prefix RBF and Kriging benchmarks demonstrate the sample efficiency of the physics-based transfer space.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Computational homogenization links a heterogeneous microstructure to an
effective macroscopic constitutive or transport response through the solution
of a microscopic boundary-value problem. For a single material configuration,
this cost can be acceptable. In many-query settings, however, the dominant
burden is often the repeated solution of the same resolved microstructure for
many admissible combinations of constituent properties. Such situations arise
in material screening, sensitivity analysis \cite{kaminski2003sensitivity},
uncertainty propagation, inverse identification, constitutive calibration,
high-fidelity database generation, optimization, and nested multiscale
calculations such as FE\textsuperscript{2} \cite{feyel2000fe2}. In these
applications, the geometry of the periodic cell may remain unchanged while
hundreds or thousands of constitutive parameter sets must be evaluated.

For periodic media represented on regular grids, FFT-based methods provide an
efficient full-field route to homogenization. Starting from the classical
scheme of Moulinec and Suquet \cite{moulinec1998numerical}, accelerated
polarization-based iterations \cite{eyre1999fast,michel2001augmented} and
variational Fourier--Galerkin formulations
\cite{vondrejc2014fft,vondrejc2015bounds} have substantially improved the
efficiency, robustness, and mathematical interpretation of periodic cell
solvers. Nevertheless, these advances primarily reduce the cost of an
individual full-order solve. If the microstructure is fixed but its local
constitutive properties are changed repeatedly, each new material point still
requires the solution of a high-dimensional microscopic problem.

Projection-based model reduction addresses this repeated cost by exploiting the low-dimensional structure of families of microscopic solutions. Reduced-basis and POD approaches have therefore been developed for computational homogenization in a variety of linear and nonlinear settings \cite{boyaval2008homogenization,yvonnet2007r3m,fritzen2013hybrid, hernandez2014highperformance,kunc2019finite,pham2025rbhm}, while transformation-field and clustering approaches provide alternative reduced descriptions of microscopic states \cite{michel2003ntfa,michel2016variational,liu2016sca}. Classical
reduced-basis methodology also established offline--online decomposition and
a posteriori error control for affinely parametrized coercive problems
\cite{prudhomme2002reliable,quarteroni2011certified}, with certification
strategies later adapted to computational homogenization and parametrized
elasticity \cite{kerfriden2014cre,hoang2016twofield}. Related developments include successive-constraint and hierarchical error estimators \cite{huynh2007scm,hain2019hierarchical} and reduced static-condensation formulations for parametrized component problems \cite{huynh2013scrbe,eftang2013port,smetana2015certification}. These works establish that parametric microscopic solution families can often be represented in spaces substantially smaller than the underlying discretized problem.

A complementary direction reduces the spatial complexity of FFT homogenization itself. Sparse sampling, low-rank tensor approximations, and tensor-train Fourier representations decrease the computational or storage requirements of the full-field solver \cite{kochmann2019sparsefft,vondrejc2020lowrank,hauck2026sfft}. At the same time, non-intrusive reduced models have combined POD with regression to learn effective constitutive responses from microscopic simulations under varying material or geometric parameters \cite{guo2021constitutive,guo2024geometric}, and sensitivity-based reduction has recently been explored for nonlinear hyperelastic homogenization \cite{lukes2026sensitivity}. These approaches address important aspects of spatial reduction, parametric approximation, or direct response prediction, but the fixed-geometry many-material setting introduces a specific form of redundancy: phase distribution, interfaces, local orientations, and discretization remain unchanged while only the constitutive properties vary. This suggests that the geometry-dependent contribution to the cell operators should be isolated once and reused across material queries. At the same time, an effective many-query formulation should remove voxel-scale operations from the deployed query, retain the variational structure of the microscopic problem rather than relying exclusively on regression of the homogenized output, and distinguish adaptive model construction from genuinely independent final assessment.


The proposed framework addresses these requirements by recasting repeated
homogenization of a fixed periodic microstructure as a reusable Ritz--Schur
constitutive transfer problem. The macroscopic loading cases are collected
into a common microscopic response matrix, while a finite affine
representation separates the queried constitutive coefficients from the fixed
spatial information of the microstructure, allowing the geometry-dependent
operator contributions to be precomputed. Since this separation alone does
not eliminate the microscopic solve, full-order responses sampled along a
fixed Sobol sequence are used to construct a full-rank POD basis that retains
the complete snapshot span and defines the corresponding Ritz trial space
without prescribing a reduced dimension, energy-retention threshold, or
numerical-rank truncation. Projection of the affine operators onto this space produces a
compact transfer model for which a new material query requires only
constitutive-coefficient evaluation, reduced assembly, and a small dense
solve, with no further Fourier--Galerkin iteration or voxel-scale field
reconstruction. The resulting reduced Schur operator remains variationally
consistent with the full-order problem, yielding in exact arithmetic an
energy identity for the reduced--full-order difference. Because each Sobol
enrichment appends columns without removing any previous snapshot direction,
the trial spaces are nested and the effective operators converge
monotonically in the Loewner sense. The offline construction is controlled by
an independent monitor set, whereas a separate space-filling design is used
only after the model has been frozen; RBF and Kriging surrogates trained on
the same material prefixes provide data-only references. Within the
assumptions of linearity, symmetry, coercivity, and finite affine constitutive
separability, the framework is intended for repeated evaluations in material
screening, sensitivity and uncertainty analysis, inverse identification and
calibration, optimization, effective-property database generation, and
multiscale workflows, and the same algebra applies to conduction, diffusion,
dielectric, Darcy, and elasticity cell problems. The numerical assessment
specializes the formulation to three-dimensional linear elasticity in
short-fiber composites.


The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:homogenization} develops the general periodic cell problem,
its discrete Schur representation, and the geometry--material affine
separation. Section~\ref{sec:transfer} introduces the Sobol--POD Ritz transfer
operator, its variational properties, and the adaptive monitoring and
held-out validation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:numerical_examples}
specializes the formulation to three-dimensional elasticity and presents the
numerical assessment. Supporting derivations, constitutive details,
computational scaling, and reproducibility information are provided in the
appendices.

\section{General parametric periodic homogenization}
\label{sec:homogenization}

\subsection{Periodic cell problem and effective operator}
\label{subsec:cell_problem}

Let \(\Omega\) be a periodic representative cell with fixed microstructure
\(\G\), and let \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Xi\) denote the queried constitutive
parameters, where \(\Xi\) is the admissible constitutive-parameter domain.

The macroscopic loading is represented by \(g\in\R^m\), where \(m\) is the
number of independent loading directions and \(g_i\) is the amplitude of the
\(i\)-th unit loading state \(\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i\). An arbitrary
macroscopic loading is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:macro_field}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
g_i\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i.
\end{equation}

The field in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field} represents the macroscopic loading
applied to the periodic cell. In a heterogeneous microstructure, the local
field is generally nonuniform because the constituent phases have different
properties. For example, in linear elasticity, an imposed macroscopic strain
is redistributed differently between the matrix and fibers.

Let \(\boldsymbol x\in\Omega\) denote the spatial position within the cell.
The microscopic redistribution is described by a periodic fluctuation
\(u(\boldsymbol x)\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}\), whose mean is fixed to zero:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zero_mean}
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\int_{\Omega}
u(\boldsymbol x)\,
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x
=
0.
\end{equation}
Here, \(|\Omega|\) is the cell volume. Periodicity ensures compatibility
between opposite faces and that the fluctuation does not alter the imposed
macroscopic average.

The local field is written as the macroscopic field plus a microscopic
correction,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_field}
\boldsymbol z(\boldsymbol x;g)
=
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u(\boldsymbol x),
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal L\) maps the fluctuation to the corresponding physical field.
For scalar transport, \(\mathcal L=\nabla\), whereas for linear elasticity,
\(\mathcal L=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\). In elasticity,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_field} becomes
\(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol x)
=\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(u)\), so the
local strain consists of the imposed macroscopic strain and the
microscopic correction induced by the heterogeneous microstructure.

Once the local field is defined, the material response is determined by the
symmetric positive-definite constitutive operator
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)\), where
\(\boldsymbol x\) identifies the local phase and orientation,
\(\boldsymbol\xi\) contains the queried constitutive properties, and
\(\G\) defines the fixed microstructure. In linear elasticity,
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb C\) and
\(\boldsymbol\sigma(\boldsymbol x)
=\mathbb C(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\boldsymbol x)\),
so the local stiffness maps the redistributed strain to the corresponding
stress.

For a prescribed macroscopic loading \(g\), the equilibrium fluctuation
\(u_g\) is determined from the weak cell problem
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:general_cell_problem}
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L v)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u_g
\right]
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x
=
0
\qquad
\forall v\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(v\) is an admissible periodic test function. In linear elasticity,
\(\mathcal L=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\) and
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb C\), so Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem} reduces to
\(\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(v)^T\boldsymbol{\sigma}
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x=0\), the weak form of mechanical equilibrium; in
the absence of body forces, this
corresponds to \(\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0\).

Once \(u_g\) is obtained, the heterogeneous cell is represented by an
effective macroscopic operator \(\Heff\) that preserves its minimum energy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:effective_energy_definition}
\frac{1}{2}
g^T\Heff(\boldsymbol{\xi};\G)g
=
\min_{u\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}}
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u
\right]^T
\mathbb A
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u
\right]
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x.
\end{equation}
The minimizer is the equilibrium fluctuation \(u_g\); hence, \(\Heff\)
defines the homogeneous operator with the same macroscopic energy as the
resolved heterogeneous cell.

In linear elasticity, \(\Heff=C^{\mathrm{eff}}\). The corresponding
quantities for the other linear problems considered here are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:general_physics}. For transport laws conventionally written
with a minus sign, such as Fourier, Fick, and Darcy laws, the sign is absorbed
into the definition of the macroscopic driving so that \(\Heff\) remains
positive definite.

problem with positive local permeability.

\subsection{Discrete energy and effective Schur map}
\label{subsec:schur_map}

We now discretize Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} to obtain a
finite-dimensional algebraic problem. The fluctuation field
\(u(\boldsymbol x)\) is represented by \(u\in\R^n\), where \(n\) is the
number of microscopic degrees of freedom. The resulting quadratic energy is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:discrete_energy}
\mathcal E(u,g;\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\frac{1}{2}u^T K(\boldsymbol\xi)u
-
u^T B(\boldsymbol\xi)g
+
\frac{1}{2}g^T D(\boldsymbol\xi)g.
\end{equation}
Here, \(K\in\R^{n\times n}\) governs the microscopic fluctuation,
\(B\in\R^{n\times m}\) couples the microscopic and macroscopic fields, and
\(D\in\R^{m\times m}\) represents the direct macroscopic energy contribution.
The derivation from Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}.

Minimization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} with respect to \(u\) gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stationarity}
K(\boldsymbol\xi)u
=
B(\boldsymbol\xi)g,
\end{equation}
where \(B\) follows the sign convention adopted in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy}.

The responses to the \(m\) independent unit macroscopic loading states are
collected in \(X=[x_1,\ldots,x_m]\in\R^{n\times m}\). They satisfy the
full-order response problem
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fom_response}
\boxed{
K(\boldsymbol\xi)X(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
B(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
Each column of \(X\) is the microscopic fluctuation associated with one unit
loading state. Consequently, \(X=K^{-1}B\), and the response to an arbitrary
loading vector \(g\) is \(u=Xg\).

Substituting the stationary response \(u=Xg\), with \(KX=B\), into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} eliminates the microscopic degrees of freedom
and yields the effective Schur map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schur_transfer}
\boxed{
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B(\boldsymbol\xi)^T X(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B(\boldsymbol\xi)^T
K(\boldsymbol\xi)^{-1}
B(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
The complete derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} from the continuous
effective-energy formulation is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}.

This Schur map condenses the \(n\)-dimensional microscopic problem into the
\(m\times m\) effective operator required by homogenization. In
three-dimensional linear elasticity, \(m=6\).


\subsection{Geometry--material affine separation}
\label{subsec:affine_separation}

The effective operator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} depends on the
discrete blocks \(K\), \(B\), and \(D\), which vary with the queried
constitutive parameters \(\boldsymbol\xi\). To separate this material
dependence from the fixed microstructure \(\G\), the local constitutive
operator is assumed to admit the finite affine representation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_affine}
\boxed{
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G).
}
\end{equation}
Here, \(Q\) is the number of affine terms,
\(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) contains the constitutive dependence, while
\(\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)\) contains the fixed spatial information of
the microstructure, including phase distribution and local orientations.

In linear elasticity, the coefficients \(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) are
obtained from the material properties of the matrix and fibers, whereas the
fields \(\mathbb A_q\) describe their spatial distribution and orientation.
For the constitutive family considered in this work, this separation is
exact; its elasticity-specific construction is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:affine}.

Because the discrete operators \(K\), \(B\), and \(D\) depend linearly on
\(\mathbb A\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} leads to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:affine_operators}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
K(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_q(\G),\\
B(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_q(\G),\\
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)D_q(\G).
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}
The derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:affine_discrete_derivation}.

For a fixed microstructure, the geometry-dependent blocks are computed once
and collected in
\(\mathcal K_{\G}=\{K_q(\G),B_q(\G),D_q(\G)\}_{q=1}^{Q}\).
A new material query changes only
\(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi)
=[\gamma_1(\boldsymbol\xi),\ldots,\gamma_Q(\boldsymbol\xi)]^T\).
Thus, the microstructure is compiled once, while each new material enters
only through its constitutive coefficients. A change in \(\G\), however,
requires a new offline compilation.


\section{Sobol--POD transfer operator}
\label{sec:transfer}

The affine separation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} isolates the material
dependence from the fixed microstructure, but it does not eliminate the
full-order microscopic solve in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response}. To remove this
cost from the online stage, full-order responses sampled over the admissible
material domain are used to construct a common Sobol--POD Ritz space. The
geometry-dependent affine operators are then projected once offline, so that
subsequent material queries require only reduced dense algebra.


\subsection{Offline construction of the response space}
\label{subsec:reduced_space}

Let
\(\Xi_C=\{\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi_C^{(N_C)}\}\subset\Xi\)
be a fixed Sobol candidate sequence. It is traversed in prefix order to form
the training set
\(\Xi_S=\{\boldsymbol\xi^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi^{(N_s)}\}\), where \(N_s\) is selected by the adaptive
procedure described in Section~\ref{subsec:heldout_validation}.

For each training material \(\boldsymbol\xi^{(j)}\),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} is solved for all \(m\) independent macroscopic
loadings, yielding \(X(\boldsymbol\xi^{(j)})\in\R^{n\times m}\). The
responses are concatenated into the snapshot matrix
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snapshot_matrix}
S
=
\left[
X(\boldsymbol\xi^{(1)}),\ldots,
X(\boldsymbol\xi^{(N_s)})
\right]
\in\R^{n\times p},
\qquad
p=mN_s.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:snapshot_matrix} shows that, although each microscopic
response belongs to the \(n\)-dimensional full-order space, the sampled
response space has dimension at most \(p\).

The POD space is constructed by the method of snapshots. Rather than
diagonalizing an \(n\times n\) correlation operator, the response
correlations are represented by the small Gram matrix
\(G_S=\langle S,S\rangle_h=W\Lambda W^T\in\R^{p\times p}\), with
\(\lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\geq\cdots\geq0\). The complete independent
snapshot span has dimension
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:full_snapshot_rank}
r
=
\operatorname{rank}(S)
=
\operatorname{rank}(G_S)
=
\#\{i:\lambda_i>0\}.
\end{equation}
Every direction in Eq.~\eqref{eq:full_snapshot_rank} is retained. The
corresponding full-rank POD/Ritz basis is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fullrank_pod}
V_r
=
S W_r\Lambda_r^{-1/2}
\in\R^{n\times r}.
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_pod},
\(W_r\in\R^{p\times r}\) and
\(\Lambda_r\in\R^{r\times r}\) contain all nonzero Gram eigendirections.
Thus, POD changes coordinates but does not truncate the sampled response
space: no prescribed reduced dimension, numerical-rank approximation, or POD
energy fraction is introduced. In floating-point arithmetic, a relative
machine-zero guard \(\tau_{\mathrm{mach}}\) is used only to avoid division by
arithmetic zero. The campaign uses \(\tau_{\mathrm{mach}}=10^{-15}\), and no
snapshot direction is removed in any of the ten final constructions.
Here, \emph{full rank} refers to the complete rank of the snapshot matrix,
not to the full microscopic dimension: \(r=\operatorname{rank}(S)\leq p\ll n\).
The online model therefore remains reduced with respect to the voxel space
while introducing no truncation inside the sampled response space.

The full voxel basis \(V_r\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_pod} need not be
formed explicitly. Defining
\(T_r=\Lambda_r^{-1/2}W_r^T\in\R^{r\times p}\), the affine operators are
first contracted in the raw snapshot coordinates as
\(\widetilde K_q=S^TK_qS\in\R^{p\times p}\) and
\(\widetilde B_q=S^TB_q\in\R^{p\times m}\). The final reduced blocks are
obtained through
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:raw_ritz_transform}
K_{q,r}
=
T_r\widetilde K_qT_r^T,
\qquad
B_{q,r}
=
T_r\widetilde B_q.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform} gives
\(K_{q,r}\in\R^{r\times r}\) and
\(B_{q,r}\in\R^{r\times m}\), and is algebraically equivalent to direct
projection with Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_pod}. The explicit construction of a
global voxel-scale basis is therefore avoided.

The complete transition from the full-order responses to the compiled
reduced transfer quantities can be summarized as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:offline_online_dimensional_flow}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Offline:}\quad
&
X(\boldsymbol\xi^{(j)})\in\R^{n\times m}
\;\longrightarrow\;
S\in\R^{n\times p}
\;\longrightarrow\;
G_S\in\R^{p\times p}
\;\longrightarrow\;
r
\\[-1mm]
&
\hspace{32mm}\longrightarrow\;
\left\{
K_{q,r}\in\R^{r\times r},
B_{q,r}\in\R^{r\times m},
D_q\in\R^{m\times m}
\right\},
\\[1mm]
\text{Online:}\quad
&
\{K_{q,r},B_{q,r},D_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}
+
\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)
\\[-1mm]
&
\hspace{15mm}\longrightarrow\;
\left\{
K_r(\boldsymbol\xi^\star),
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi^\star),
D(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)
\right\}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)
\;\longrightarrow\;
\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi^\star).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first line of Eq.~\eqref{eq:offline_online_dimensional_flow} highlights
the dimensional reduction \(n\rightarrow p\rightarrow r\), with
\(r\leq p\) and, in the targeted high-dimensional regime, \(p\ll n\).
After this offline compilation, only
\(\{K_{q,r},B_{q,r},D_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}\) is required for subsequent material
queries; the full snapshot fields are not required online.


\subsection{Reduced Ritz--Schur transfer}
\label{subsec:reduced_transfer}
The second line of Eq.~\eqref{eq:offline_online_dimensional_flow} defines the
online transfer stage. Once the reduced affine blocks have been compiled, a
new constitutive query \(\boldsymbol\xi\) enters only through the coefficients
\(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\). Because projection is linear, substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} into
\(K_r=V_r^TKV_r\) and \(B_r=V_r^TB\), together with the reduced blocks defined
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform}, gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:online_assembly}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_{q,r},\\
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_{q,r}.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:online_assembly} therefore assembles the queried reduced
operators directly from the precompiled affine blocks. The remaining block
\(D(\boldsymbol\xi)\in\R^{m\times m}\) is assembled analogously from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}.

The microscopic response is represented in the Ritz space as
\(X_r(\boldsymbol\xi)=V_r\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi)\), with
\(\widehat X_r\in\R^{r\times m}\). Substituting this representation into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} and applying Galerkin projection with \(V_r^T\)
gives

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced_response}
\boxed{
K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response},
\(K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)\in\R^{r\times r}\) and
\(B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)\in\R^{r\times m}\). Thus, for every new material, the
original \(n\)-dimensional microscopic solve is replaced by a dense reduced
system of dimension \(r\), with \(r\ll n\).

Using \(B_r=V_r^TB\) and \(X_r=V_r\widehat X_r\), the solution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response} defines the reduced effective operator through
the Schur structure inherited from Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced_transfer}
\boxed{
\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)^T
\widehat X_r(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer} therefore maps each queried material
directly to its effective operator without reconstructing the full
microscopic response.

Once the reduced affine blocks used in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly}--\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer} have been
compiled, a standard material query requires no full-dimensional field
operation. For fixed \(m\), the leading online cost is
\(\mathcal O(Qr^2+r^3)\), independent of the voxel count
\(N_{\mathrm{vox}}\), whereas one full-order Fourier--Galerkin material
evaluation scales as
\(\mathcal O(mI_{\mathrm{CG}}N_{\mathrm{vox}}\log N_{\mathrm{vox}})\).
Detailed offline, storage, and online complexity estimates are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:computational_details}.

Beyond the reduction in online computational cost, the Ritz construction
preserves the variational structure of the underlying full-order problem.
To characterize this property, define the microscopic response error
\(E_X=X-X_r\) and the associated residual \(R=B-KX_r\). The difference
between the reduced and full-order effective operators then satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_schur_identity}
\boxed{
\Hr-\Hfom
=
E_X^TKE_X
=
R^TK^{-1}R
\succeq0.
}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity} shows that the reduced Schur operator
provides an upper bound to the full-order operator in the Loewner sense, while
their difference is exactly the \(K\)-energy of the microscopic Ritz error.

This variational property also induces an ordering between reduced operators
constructed on nested trial spaces. If
\(\operatorname{span}(V_r)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(V_s)\), then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}
\boxed{
\Hfom
\preceq
\Hs
\preceq
\Hr.
}
\end{equation}
In the present full-rank construction, nesting is automatic. If \(S_\ell\)
denotes the snapshot matrix after \(\ell\) materials, appending the next
response block gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fullrank_nested_spaces}
\operatorname{range}(S_\ell)
\subseteq
\operatorname{range}(S_{\ell+1}).
\end{equation}
Consequently, for every material query,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur}
\Hfom
\preceq
H_{\ell+1}^{\mathrm{eff}}
\preceq
H_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur} is the monotone Loewner
convergence property enabled by retaining the complete snapshot span. Since
both operator differences are positive semidefinite, it also implies
\(\|H_{\ell+1}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\Hfom\|_F
\leq\|H_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\Hfom\|_F\) in exact arithmetic. Hence each
monitor error, and therefore their maximum, is nonincreasing under exact
full-rank Ritz enrichment. The derivation of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity} and
\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering} is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:ritz_schur}. Finite-precision contractions and iterative
FOM references may introduce small departures from these exact identities;
the reported monitor history and Schur diagnostic quantify the realized
implementation.

\subsection{Adaptive training, monitoring, and held-out validation}
\label{subsec:heldout_validation}

The Sobol training length \(N_s\) is selected adaptively using an independent
monitor set
\(\Xi_M=\{\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(N_M)}\}\). The corresponding FOM effective operators are
computed once, while the monitor fields are never included in the snapshot
matrix of Eq.~\eqref{eq:snapshot_matrix}. After \(\ell\) Sobol materials
have been incorporated, the reduced space and affine blocks are updated
according to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:full_snapshot_rank} and
\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform}, and the worst-case monitor error is evaluated as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:monitor_stopping}
e_M^{(\ell)}
=
\max_{\boldsymbol\xi\in\Xi_M}
\frac{
\left\|
H_r^{\mathrm{eff},(\ell)}(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi)
\right\|_F
}{
\left\|
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi)
\right\|_F
}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:monitor_stopping} is used only to control enrichment; the
monitor materials do not modify the Sobol sequence or snapshot space.

Monitoring begins at a prescribed minimum prefix length \(\ell_{\min}\).
The selected training size is the first Sobol prefix satisfying the target
accuracy,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:adaptive_stop}
N_s
=
\min\left\{
\ell\in\{\ell_{\min},\ldots,N_C\}:
e_M^{(\ell)}
\leq
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ROM}}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:adaptive_stop} therefore determines the training set
\(\Xi_S\) used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:snapshot_matrix}. If no admissible prefix
satisfies the tolerance, the target is reported as unmet.
Because it uses only five finite monitor points, this first crossing remains
a heuristic stopping criterion rather than a uniform error bound over
\(\Xi\). Within the exact full-rank Ritz algebra it is stable under further
enrichment by Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur}; in the implementation,
its realized behavior is also checked directly from the recorded monitor
history.

After \(N_s\) has been selected, the reduced operators are frozen and an
independent held-out set
\(\Xi_V=\{\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(1)},\ldots,
\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(N_V)}\}\) is used exclusively for final assessment. For
each validation material, the relative tensor error is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:validation_error}
e_{V,j}
=
\frac{
\left\|
\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(j)})
-
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(j)})
\right\|_F
}{
\left\|
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(j)})
\right\|_F
},
\qquad
j=1,\ldots,N_V.
\end{equation}
Because the validation FOM responses are evaluated only after the ROM has
been frozen, Eq.~\eqref{eq:validation_error} provides an independent
assessment that does not influence enrichment, span construction, or stopping.
For the numerical campaign, \(\Xi_V\) is a deterministic space-filling
design: the affine coefficient vectors of an independent Sobol pool are
normalized coordinatewise, and a maximin traversal selects the final points.
Consequently, the reported pass fraction describes this finite design and is
neither an i.i.d. estimate nor a probabilistic certificate over \(\Xi\).

The implementation also records the smallest eigenvalues of the symmetric
parts of \(K_r\) and \(\Cr\), together with the spectral SPD margin
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spectral_spd_margin}
\rho_K
=
\frac{\lambda_{\min}[\operatorname{sym}(K_r)]}
{\lambda_{\max}[\operatorname{sym}(K_r)]},
\qquad
\operatorname{sym}(A)=\frac{A+A^T}{2}.
\end{equation}
This ratio is reported as a margin, not as a condition number. The numerical
Ritz--Schur diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schur_eta}
\eta_{\mathrm{Schur}}
=
\frac{
\lambda_{\min}\!\left[
\operatorname{sym}(\Cr-\Cfom)
\right]
}{\|\Cfom\|_2}.
\end{equation}
Exact arithmetic gives \(\eta_{\mathrm{Schur}}\geq0\) by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity}; a negative measured value therefore
quantifies the combined finite-precision and iterative-solver deviation from
the ideal Ritz identity.


\subsection{Offline--online workflow}
\label{subsec:offline_online}

The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:offline_online}.
For each fixed microstructure \(\G\), all Fourier--Galerkin solves, snapshot
generation, full-span orthonormalization, adaptive monitoring, and affine
Ritz contractions are confined to the offline stage. Once the reduced
operators have been frozen, a new material query requires only evaluation of
\(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\), assembly of \(K_r\) and \(B_r\)
through Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly} and of \(D\) through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}, solution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response}, and evaluation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer}. No full-order solve or voxel-scale field
reconstruction is required online.

\section{Numerical examples}
\label{sec:numerical_examples}

The proposed Sobol--POD transfer formulation is assessed through
three-dimensional linear elasticity over a set of periodic composite
microstructures. The numerical examples verify the FFTHomPy adaptations and
voxel resolution, examine adaptive construction and held-out accuracy,
compare the transfer model with data-only surrogates, and measure the
computational benefit for repeated constitutive queries.


\subsection{Elasticity specialization and numerical setup}
\label{subsec:elasticity_setup}

For three-dimensional linear elasticity, the general formulation introduced
in Section~\ref{sec:homogenization} specializes as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elastic_specialization}
m=6,
\qquad
\mathcal L u
=
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(u),
\qquad
\mathbb A
=
\mathbb C,
\qquad
\Heff
=
\Ceff.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:elastic_specialization} implies
\(X\in\R^{n\times6}\), \(\Hfom=\Cfom\), and \(\Hr=\Cr\). Accordingly, the

\(\Cfom=D-B^TK^{-1}B\).

The constitutive family consists of an isotropic matrix and transversely

For each geometry, a Sobol candidate sequence containing
\(N_C=1024\) constitutive points is traversed from
\(\ell_{\min}=2\). Adaptive stopping is controlled by
\(N_M=5\) independent monitor materials with target tolerance
\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ROM}}=10^{-4}\). After the reduced operator is

G00--G09, yielding 200 material--geometry comparisons. Seeds and complete
generation settings are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_reproducibility}.

Training, monitoring, and FOM timing use Fourier--Galerkin/CG in
\texttt{float32} with relative tolerance \(10^{-5}\). Held-out references use
\texttt{float64} with relative tolerance \(10^{-6}\), initialized by the
corresponding converged timing field; the refinement time is excluded from
\(t_{\mathrm{FOM}}\). Thus, the more accurate held-out solve is used only as
an error reference and does not alter the nominal reduced model. Raw
snapshots and voxel-scale affine Ritz actions use \texttt{float32}. The
snapshot Gram products are evaluated in \texttt{float64} on the CPU, while
the reduced accumulation, Gram transformation, and online dense solves use
\texttt{float64}. This hybrid path avoids the low FP64 throughput of the
consumer GPU without evaluating an ill-conditioned full-rank Gram product in
\texttt{float32}. The machine-zero guard is
\(\tau_{\mathrm{mach}}=10^{-15}\); it removes no snapshot direction in the
reported campaign, so \(r=6N_s\) for every final ROM. No SPD probe,
automatic rebuild, or diagonal regularization is used.
Complete implementation, memory, sampling, and reproducibility settings are
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_reproducibility}.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A common Sobol--POD transfer formulation has been developed for
repeated periodic homogenization with fixed geometry and varying local
constitutive properties. The construction combines a Schur representation of
the effective operator, an affine geometry--material separation, and a
full-rank POD Ritz representation of the complete snapshot span.
In exact arithmetic, the Ritz restriction preserves the variational
structure of the Schur map: the ROM--FOM operator difference is positive
semidefinite and is exactly the \(K\)-energy of the microscopic response
error. Since enrichment appends snapshots without truncation, the reduced
spaces are nested and the effective operators are ordered monotonically
toward the full-order operator. The nominal hybrid contractions are assessed
numerically and are not treated as a floating-point certificate of the exact
identity.

The formulation is not restricted to elasticity. It applies directly to
linear, symmetric, and coercive periodic cell problems such as thermal and
electrical conduction, mass diffusion, dielectric electrostatics, linear
Darcy transport, and linear elasticity whenever the local constitutive law
admits the required finite separable representation. The number of
macroscopic loadings changes with the physics, but the transfer algebra
remains unchanged.

Geometry is handled implicitly through the full-order operators and snapshots
used by the small-Gram POD construction. Consequently, no
morphology-specific reduced descriptor is required. The framework is
independent of the geometry class, while each compiled operator remains
specific to the fixed geometry from which it was constructed.

The ten three-dimensional elasticity examples selected Sobol prefixes between
6 and 16 materials and full snapshot ranks between 36 and 96. On the
independent 20-point affine-maximin design, 91 of 200 material--geometry pairs
satisfied the \(10^{-4}\) relative Frobenius target and 198 satisfied
\(10^{-3}\); the mean and maximum errors were \(2.134\times10^{-4}\) and
\(1.297\times10^{-3}\). Thus, stopping at the
first crossing over five monitors does not certify the same tolerance on a
wider space-filling design. No held-out point was used for subsequent
enrichment, and every reduced system and stiffness tensor remained positive
definite. The raw Schur diagnostic is retained as a secondary record of the
finite-precision limit of the exact variational ordering.

At the same geometry-wise Sobol prefixes, the physics-based transfer model
outperformed direct RBF and Kriging surrogates in all 200 paired comparisons.
The training-only-tuned baseline mean errors, \(6.256\times10^{-2}\) and
\(7.585\times10^{-2}\), were approximately 293 and 355 times the
Sobol--POD mean error. This comparison isolates the number of full-order
training materials while recognizing that the transfer construction retains
six microscopic fields per material and the data-only surrogates retain only
the effective tensor.

Across G00--G09, median FOM material times ranged from
\(0.138\) to \(7.442~\si{\second}\), whereas synchronized CuPy/CUDA batches
required 2.33--7.83~\si{\micro\second} per material after amortization. The
corresponding throughput speed-ups were \(2.59\times10^4\) to
\(1.27\times10^6\), and the measured compilation costs were recovered after
15--37 material queries.

The resolution study further showed that voxel refinement is most important
for the dense long-fiber geometry: its tensor error relative to the
result at 6 voxels per fiber diameter decreased from \(5.38\%\) at resolution
3 to \(0.91\%\) at resolution 5. This discretization effect is distinct from
ROM error and should be considered when the compiled operator is used as a
high-accuracy surrogate of the underlying microstructure.

The three-dimensional elasticity specialization illustrates how the abstract
operators become stiffness tensors, Mandel rotations, and seven affine
constitutive coefficients. Extensions to the scalar
transport problems listed in Table~\ref{tab:general_physics} require only the
corresponding local constitutive operator, macroscopic loading basis, affine
coefficient map, and validation protocol. Mixed pore-scale flow and
nonlinear problems require additional stability or nonlinear reduction
machinery and remain outside the direct scope of the present formulation.

\section{Derivation of the discrete Schur representation}
\label{app:schur_derivation}

Starting from the effective-energy definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition}, expansion of the quadratic
integrand separates the microscopic, coupling, and macroscopic contributions,
\[
\mathcal E
=
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L u)^T\mathbb A(\mathcal L u)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x
+
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L u)^T\mathbb A\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x
+
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)^T
\mathbb A
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x.
\]

After spatial discretization, write
\(u(\boldsymbol x)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}u_a\phi_a(\boldsymbol x)\).
Using the macroscopic loading expansion in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field}, the
three discrete blocks are
\begin{align}
K_{ab}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\\
B_{ai}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
-
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\\
D_{ij}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\int_{\Omega}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_j
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\end{align}
with \(a,b=1,\ldots,n\) and \(i,j=1,\ldots,m\). These definitions give the
discrete energy in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy}; the minus sign in \(B\)
accounts for the cross-energy convention used there.

Stationarity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} with respect to \(u\) gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity}. Collecting the \(m\) unit-load responses then
gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response}, so \(X=K^{-1}B\) and \(u=Xg\).
Substitution into the discrete energy yields
\[
\mathcal E(Xg,g)
=
\frac{1}{2}g^TX^TKXg
-
g^TX^TBg
+
\frac{1}{2}g^TDg.
\]
Using \(KX=B\) and \(K=K^T\) gives
\(X^TKX=X^TB=B^TX\), and therefore
\[
\mathcal E(Xg,g)
=
\frac{1}{2}
g^T
\left[
D-B^TX
\right]
g.
\]
Comparison with Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} proves the Schur
representation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer}.

\section{Derivation of the affine discrete operators}
\label{app:affine_discrete_derivation}

The discrete affine operators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} follow from
the local separation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} and the block definitions
in Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}. For example, substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} into the definition of \(K_{ab}\) gives
\[
K_{ab}(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x.
\]
Because \(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) is independent of
\(\boldsymbol x\), the remaining integral defines
\[
(K_q)_{ab}
=
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\]
and hence
\(K(\boldsymbol\xi)=\sum_q\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_q(\G)\).

Applying the same substitution to the \(B\) and \(D\) definitions in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation} gives
\[
B(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_q(\G),
\qquad
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)D_q(\G),
\]
which completes the derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}.

\section{Ritz--Schur identity and variational ordering}
\label{app:ritz_schur}

This appendix proves the structure-preserving property stated in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity} and
\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}. Consider one fixed material point
\(\boldsymbol\xi\) and suppress its dependence for readability. Let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_error_residual}
E_X=X-X_r,
\qquad
R=B-KX_r.
\end{equation}
Using the full-order equation \(KX=B\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_error_relation}
R
=
KX-KX_r
=
K E_X.
\end{equation}
Since \(K\) is invertible on the constrained periodic space,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:error_from_residual}
E_X=K^{-1}R.
\end{equation}

From the full-order and reduced Schur maps,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} and \eqref{eq:reduced_transfer},
\begin{align}
\Hr-\Hfom
&=
\left(D-B^TX_r\right)
-
\left(D-B^TX\right)
\nonumber\\
&=
B^T(X-X_r)
=
B^TE_X.
\label{eq:schur_difference_first}
\end{align}
Because \(B=KX=K(X_r+E_X)\) and \(K=K^T\),
\begin{align}
B^TE_X
&=
(X_r+E_X)^T K E_X
\nonumber\\
&=
X_r^T K E_X
+
E_X^T K E_X.
\label{eq:schur_difference_expand}
\end{align}

The Galerkin equation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:galerkin_residual_orthogonality}
V_r^T(KX_r-B)=0,
\end{equation}
or, with the residual definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_error_residual},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:galerkin_residual_zero}
V_r^TR=0.
\end{equation}
Using \(X_r=V_r\widehat X_r\) and
\(K E_X=R\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual_error_relation},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cross_term_zero}
X_r^T K E_X
=
\widehat X_r^T V_r^T R
=
0.
\end{equation}
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_difference_expand} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_energy_identity_appendix}
\Hr-\Hfom
=
E_X^T K E_X.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_from_residual} and the symmetry of \(K\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_energy_identity_appendix}
E_X^T K E_X
=
R^T K^{-1}R.
\end{equation}
Finally, for any \(g\in\R^m\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_psd_proof}
g^T(\Hr-\Hfom)g
=
(E_Xg)^T K(E_Xg)
\geq 0,
\end{equation}
because \(K\) is positive definite. Therefore,
\[
\Hr-\Hfom\succeq0,
\]
which proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity}.

The nested-space ordering follows directly from the same variational
structure. For a fixed macroscopic loading \(g\), the full-order solution
minimizes the discrete energy over the complete admissible microscopic space,
whereas the Ritz solutions minimize the same energy over their respective
trial spaces. Thus,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:restricted_energy_minima}
\frac{1}{2}g^T\Hfom g
=
\min_{u\in\R^n}\mathcal E(u,g),
\qquad
\frac{1}{2}g^T\Hr g
=
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_r)}\mathcal E(u,g),
\end{equation}
with the understood periodic constraint already incorporated in the discrete
space. If
\(\operatorname{span}(V_r)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(V_s)\), then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nested_minima}
\min_{u\in\R^n}\mathcal E(u,g)
\leq
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_s)}\mathcal E(u,g)
\leq
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_r)}\mathcal E(u,g).
\end{equation}
Hence, for every \(g\in\R^m\),
\[
g^T\Hfom g
\leq
g^T\Hs g
\leq
g^T\Hr g,
\]
which is equivalent to the Loewner ordering
\[
\Hfom
\preceq
\Hs
\preceq
\Hr.
\]
This proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}. In the present full-rank
construction, the trial spaces are precisely
\(\operatorname{range}(S_\ell)\). Appending a new response block preserves
every previous column, so
\(\operatorname{range}(S_\ell)\subseteq
\operatorname{range}(S_{\ell+1})\) without an additional assumption. Taking
\(V_r\) and \(V_s\) as any orthonormal coordinates for these two spans gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_monotone_schur}. Moreover, if
\(A=H_{\ell+1}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\Hfom\) and
\(B=H_\ell^{\mathrm{eff}}-\Hfom\), then
\(0\preceq A\preceq B\). Monotonicity of unitarily invariant norms on the
positive-semidefinite cone gives \(\|A\|_F\leq\|B\|_F\), establishing the
exact nonincrease of each monitor error under enrichment.

\(\chi_m(\boldsymbol x)\) and \(\chi_f(\boldsymbol x)\) denote the matrix and
fiber indicator functions, with \(\chi_m+\chi_f=1\).

For the isotropic matrix, the Lam\'e parameters are
\[
\mu_m
=
\frac{E_m}{2(1+\nu_m)},
\qquad
\lambda_m
=
\frac{E_m\nu_m}{(1+\nu_m)(1-2\nu_m)}.

+

=

\qquad

=

\((11,22,33,\sqrt{2}\,23,\sqrt{2}\,13,\sqrt{2}\,12)\), the local fiber

=
\begin{bmatrix}

&

\\[2mm]

The coefficients \(c_j(\boldsymbol\xi)\) follow directly from the inverse of

C_f^{\mathrm{loc}}
T(\boldsymbol x;\G)^T.
\end{equation}

separation defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_affine_expansion},
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

=

\section{Computational scaling and numerical implementation}
\label{app:computational_details}

This appendix collects the implementation-level costs omitted from the main
methodological development. The purpose is to separate the asymptotic cost of
the Fourier--Galerkin full-order model from the offline contractions required
to compile the ROM and from the final online query.

Let
\[
N_{\mathrm{vox}}=N_xN_yN_z
\]
be the total number of voxels in the periodic grid, let
\(p_\ell=m\ell\) be the number of raw snapshot columns after \(\ell\)
training materials, and let \(p=mN_s\) be the final raw snapshot dimension.
The full snapshot rank after enrichment \(\ell\) is denoted by
\(r_\ell\leq p_\ell\), with final rank \(r=r_{N_s}\).

For the Fourier--Galerkin/CG solver, one application of the discrete operator
requires a fixed number of FFTs. Since each FFT scales as
\(\mathcal O(N_{\mathrm{vox}}\log N_{\mathrm{vox}})\), the leading cost of
one material with all \(m\) independent loading cases is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{FOM}}
=
\mathcal O\!\left(
mI_{\mathrm{CG}}N_{\mathrm{vox}}\log N_{\mathrm{vox}}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(I_{\mathrm{CG}}\) denotes the representative CG iteration count. For
fixed physics, vector/tensor component counts contribute only constant
factors, and the FFT workspace scales as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{FOM}}
=
\mathcal O(mN_{\mathrm{vox}}).
\end{equation}

The raw snapshots are retained only until the reduced operators have been
frozen. Their storage therefore scales as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O(N_{\mathrm{vox}}p).
\end{equation}
Incremental Gram and affine Ritz contractions over the complete Sobol prefix
accumulate to
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O\!\left((Q+1)N_{\mathrm{vox}}p^2\right)
\end{equation}
work, with retained small-block storage
\(\mathcal O((Q+1)p^2)\) in addition to the snapshots. Repeated eigendecomposition
of the Gram matrix and the raw-to-Ritz transformations in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform} involve only the snapshot coordinates. A
conservative bound for the dense algebra accumulated over adaptive enrichment
is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O\!\left((Q+1)
\sum_{\ell=\ell_{\min}}^{N_s}p_\ell^3\right).
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the only cubic offline dependence is on the small raw snapshot
dimension \(p_\ell\), not on the voxel count.

For one reduced material query, retaining the loading dimension explicitly,
\begin{align}
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{ROM}}(r,m,Q)
&=
\mathcal O\!\left[
Q(r^2+rm+m^2)+r^3+r^2m+rm^2
\right],
\label{eq:rom_time_complexity_appendix}\\
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{ROM}}(r,m)
&=
\mathcal O(r^2+rm+m^2).
\label{eq:rom_memory_complexity_appendix}
\end{align}
For fixed \(m\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:rom_time_complexity_appendix} reduces to the
leading dependence \(\mathcal O(Qr^2+r^3)\). The stored reduced model contains
the projected affine blocks and the small \(D_q\) contributions, requiring
\begin{equation}
\mathcal O\!\left[Q(r^2+rm+m^2)\right]
\end{equation}
memory.

The nominal campaign performs \(N_s+N_M+N_V\) high-fidelity material solves:
only the
\(N_s\) training materials contribute snapshots, the \(N_M\) monitor
materials control the stopping point, and the \(N_V\) validation materials

The implementation bounds temporary memory without changing the resolved
snapshot span. Raw snapshot fields are stored in a preallocated contiguous
array at the configured storage precision and, when applicable, are ordered
by phase and local orientation. Affine Ritz products are streamed in
phase-supported coefficient blocks on the GPU, while the Gram products are
formed incrementally on the CPU; the resulting small blocks are accumulated
at the configured reduced-algebra precision.
Only the Gram eigendecomposition and the transformations in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:raw_ritz_transform} are repeated after each enrichment; no
global POD projection pass is required. The coefficient-block size is chosen
from the available memory, and the online batch size is reduced as necessary
to respect the configured workspace cap.

The contraction precision and machine-zero guard are numerical configuration
parameters. The guard is not an approximation tolerance: it only prevents
division by arithmetic zero in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_pod}. No direction is
discarded in the reported campaign, for which \(r=p\) at every accepted
prefix. The nominal workflow does not probe, regularize, or rebuild a frozen
ROM. A failed reduced evaluation is recorded as a numerical failure.
The concrete precisions, tolerances, and memory limits used in the numerical
campaign are reported in Table~\ref{tab:campaign_protocol}.

After compilation and validation, full voxel fields are transient and are not
required by the deployed transfer model. Thus the online dependence is on
\(Q\), \(r\), and \(m\), whereas \(N_{\mathrm{vox}}\) appears only in the
offline Fourier--Galerkin and contraction stages. In particular, no
\(N_{\mathrm{vox}}^3\) operation is introduced by the method; the cubic
terms are confined to the reduced or snapshot coordinates \(r\) and \(p\).

\section{Training-only surrogate hyperparameter optimization}
\label{app:surrogate_hyperparameters}

The RBF and Kriging comparisons use the same Sobol prefix available to the
transfer model at each training size. Inputs are the seven affine
constitutive coefficients, normalized coordinatewise using ranges computed
from the fixed 1024-point candidate sequence. Outputs are the 21 independent
upper-triangular entries of the symmetric Mandel stiffness tensor and are
standardized using only the current training prefix.

For RBF, deterministic leave-one-out cross-validation minimizes the mean
relative Frobenius tensor error over eight kernel families: linear,
thin-plate, cubic, quintic, multiquadric, inverse-multiquadric,
inverse-quadratic, and Gaussian. The shape parameter spans 16 logarithmically
spaced multiples from \(2^{-12}\) to \(2^3\) of the inverse median pair
distance. Five smoothing levels from zero to \(10^{-1}\) and every polynomial
degree admissible at the current fold size are also considered.

For Kriging, the covariance family is selected by maximum optimized training
log marginal likelihood among ten isotropic and
automatic-relevance-determination variants of Mat\'ern,
squared-exponential, rational-quadratic, and Mat\'ern-plus-linear kernels.
Covariance amplitude, length scales, nugget, and the rational-quadratic shape
parameter when applicable are optimized continuously. Every candidate uses
two deterministic optimizer restarts during family selection; the winning
family is then refitted with ten deterministic restarts. This complete search
is repeated for every geometry and prefix. Monitor and held-out responses are
excluded from all hyperparameter decisions.
