\begin{abstract}
Repeated computational homogenization becomes expensive when the same
microstructure must be solved for many different local constitutive
properties. This work formulates that setting as a reusable transfer problem
for a fixed periodic microstructure. The formulation applies to linear,
symmetric, and coercive periodic cell problems with separable constitutive
dependence, including thermal and electrical conduction, mass diffusion,
dielectric electrostatics, linear Darcy transport, and linear elasticity.
The independent macroscopic loading problems are assembled into one discrete
Schur map, while material dependence is separated from the fixed geometry
through an affine operator representation. During the offline stage, one
fixed Sobol sequence is traversed with the full-order Fourier--Galerkin model
and the resulting microscopic responses are appended to a common snapshot
matrix. Proper orthogonal decomposition is used only to orthonormalize the
complete numerically independent snapshot span; no energy-retention threshold
or prescribed reduced dimension is introduced. The resulting Ritz model
directly approximates the effective operator and preserves the variational
ordering of the Schur map: the reduced operator bounds the full-order operator
in the Loewner sense, with an exact energy identity for their difference.
Accuracy is assessed by independent Fourier--Galerkin solves over the
admissible material space, while
the online stage uses only reduced dense systems assembled from the
precomputed affine blocks. A fixed 16-material monitor set controls sequential
enrichment, stopping at the first prefix below the target error. The frozen
model is then assessed once on a second independent 16-material set
that has no role in construction. The
elasticity specialization is stated separately to make explicit how the common
transfer algebra applies to three-dimensional stiffness tensors.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item A common transfer formulation covers linear coercive transport and elasticity cell problems.
\item Exact affine separation compiles fixed geometry while queried material enters only through constitutive coefficients.
\item Sobol sampling and numerical-rank POD retain the complete numerically resolved snapshot span.
\item Ritz--Schur projection preserves variational ordering and gives an exact energy identity for the operator error.
\item Independent FFT monitoring and final validation assess a ROM whose online stage uses only reduced dense algebra.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Computational homogenization replaces an explicit macroscopic constitutive or
transport law by the solution of a microscopic boundary-value problem. This
is attractive for heterogeneous media whose effective response depends
strongly on the resolved microstructure, but it becomes costly whenever the
same microstructure must be queried repeatedly. Such many-query situations
arise in material screening, sensitivity analysis
\cite{kaminski2003sensitivity}, uncertainty propagation, inverse
identification, and nested multiscale calculations such as
FE\textsuperscript{2} \cite{feyel2000fe2}.

For periodic media represented on regular grids, FFT-based solvers provide an
efficient full-field route to homogenization. Beginning with the classical
scheme of Moulinec and Suquet \cite{moulinec1998numerical}, accelerated
polarization-based iterations \cite{eyre1999fast,michel2001augmented} and
variational Fourier--Galerkin formulations
\cite{vondrejc2014fft,vondrejc2015bounds} improved convergence and clarified
the mathematical structure of the discrete problem. Even so, a full-order
cell solve remains high dimensional, and repeatedly solving it over a
constitutive-parameter domain can dominate the cost of a many-query study.

Projection-based reduction addresses this repeated cost by representing a
family of microscopic responses in a smaller space. POD and reduced-basis
ideas have been combined with computational homogenization in several forms
\cite{boyaval2008homogenization,yvonnet2007r3m,fritzen2013hybrid,
hernandez2014highperformance,kunc2019finite,pham2025rbhm}, while related
approaches reduce or cluster the internal state of the microstructure
\cite{michel2003ntfa,michel2016variational,liu2016sca}. FFT-specific
reductions have also been proposed
\cite{kochmann2019sparsefft,vondrejc2020lowrank,hauck2026sfft}. Together,
these methods show that the response family generated by changing local
properties can be much smaller than the underlying voxel space.

A deployed reduced model must also be checked against full-order calculations
on materials that were not used to build the basis. This is particularly
relevant in homogenization because the quantity transferred to a macroscopic
model is usually a small effective operator rather than the complete
microscopic field.

The present work starts from that output structure. For one fixed periodic
microstructure \(\G\), the independent macroscopic loading problems are
collected into a single response matrix and the effective property is written
as a Schur map. The local constitutive dependence is separated from the
spatial geometry at operator level. A prescribed Sobol set samples the
admissible constitutive domain offline, and the complete numerically
independent span of the resulting responses is retained through POD. POD is
therefore used to orthonormalize the available physical response space rather
than to introduce a second energy-truncation parameter. Because the reduced
problem is a Ritz restriction of the same quadratic energy, its Schur operator
inherits a one-sided variational ordering: the ROM--FOM operator difference is
positive semidefinite and equals the microscopic Ritz error measured in the
\(K\)-energy. For nested trial spaces, the corresponding effective operators
converge monotonically in the Loewner sense.

The formulation is morphology agnostic. Fibers, inclusions, layers, pores,
clustering, and local orientations require no special reduced ansatz because
their effect is already embedded in the full-order operators and in the
snapshots from which the SVD is computed. This does not imply a universal
basis across geometries: each fixed microstructure produces its own compiled
operator, and changing \(\G\) requires a new offline compilation.

The same algebraic construction applies to linear, symmetric, and coercive
periodic problems with separable constitutive dependence. Direct examples
include thermal conduction, electrical conduction, mass diffusion,
dielectric electrostatics, linear Darcy transport, and linear elasticity.
In three dimensions, scalar gradient-driven problems require three
independent macroscopic excitations, whereas elasticity requires six
independent strain states. Pore-resolved Stokes flow and nonlinear transport
are outside the direct scope because their mixed or nonlinear structure
requires a different stability analysis.

The second ingredient is an adaptive assessment protocol. Independent Sobol
materials are solved with the full-order FFT model and compared with the ROM
response using a relative Frobenius tensor error. A fixed monitor set controls
when sequential Sobol enrichment stops, without changing the order of the
training sequence. A second independent set is evaluated only after the ROM
has been frozen and provides the final held-out assessment.

Section~\ref{sec:homogenization} develops the common periodic cell problem and
its Schur representation. Section~\ref{sec:transfer} introduces the reduced
transfer operator, its Ritz--Schur ordering, and the validation protocol.
Section~\ref{sec:elasticity} specializes the framework to three-dimensional
elasticity. The detailed elastic coefficient map is collected in the
appendix.

\section{General parametric periodic homogenization}
\label{sec:homogenization}

\subsection{Periodic cell problem and effective operator}
\label{subsec:cell_problem}

Let \(\Omega\) be a periodic representative cell with fixed microstructure
\(\G\), and collect the queried local constitutive properties in
\[
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Xi,
\]
where \(\Xi\) denotes the admissible constitutive-parameter domain. Because
\(\G\) remains fixed during the offline compilation and all subsequent
queries, its dependence is omitted from discrete quantities unless explicitly
needed.

The macroscopic loading is represented by \(g\in\R^m\), where \(m\) is the
number of independent loading directions and \(g_i\) is the amplitude of the
\(i\)-th unit loading state \(\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i\). An arbitrary
macroscopic loading is obtained by combining these basic states. This
macroscopic field is defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:macro_field}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
g_i\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i.
\end{equation}

The field prescribed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field} represents the loading of
the cell as a whole. In a heterogeneous microstructure, however, the local
field is generally not uniform because the constituent phases have different
properties. Interfaces and local orientations can further modify this
redistribution. For example, in linear elasticity, prescribing a macroscopic
strain does not imply that the matrix and fibers experience the same local
strain; each phase deforms according to its local stiffness while remaining
mechanically compatible.

Let \(\boldsymbol x\in\Omega\) denote the spatial position within the periodic
cell. The microscopic adjustment at each position \(\boldsymbol x\) is
described by a periodic fluctuation \(u(\boldsymbol x)\in
\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}\). To remove the arbitrary constant associated with
this fluctuation, its average over the cell is fixed to zero. This condition is
stated in Eq.~\eqref{eq:zero_mean} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zero_mean}
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\int_{\Omega}
u(\boldsymbol x)\,
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x
=
0.
\end{equation}
Here, \(|\Omega|\) denotes the volume of the periodic cell. The periodicity of
\(u\) additionally ensures compatibility between opposite faces of the cell
and makes its differential contribution vanish in the macroscopic average.

The local field at position \(\boldsymbol x\) is then obtained by adding the
microscopic correction to the macroscopic field defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field}. This decomposition is written in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_field} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_field}
\boldsymbol z(\boldsymbol x;g)
=
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u(\boldsymbol x).
\end{equation}
The operator \(\mathcal L\) converts the fluctuation into the same physical
quantity as the imposed macroscopic field. For scalar transport,
\(\mathcal L=\nabla\), whereas for linear elasticity,
\(\mathcal L=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\). In the latter case,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_field} becomes
\[
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol x)
=
\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
+
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(u),
\]
which shows that the strain at each point of the matrix or fibers consists of
the imposed macroscopic strain plus a local correction caused by the
heterogeneous microstructure.

Once the local field in Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_field} is known, the constitutive
law determines how the material responds at each position
\(\boldsymbol x\). This local behavior is represented by the symmetric
positive-definite operator
\[
\mathbb A
=
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G),
\]
where the dependence on \(\boldsymbol x\) identifies the material and local
orientation present at that position, \(\boldsymbol\xi\) contains the queried
constitutive properties, and \(\G\) defines the fixed microstructure.

For example, in linear elasticity,
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb C\), and the local constitutive relation is
\[
\boldsymbol\sigma(\boldsymbol x)
=
\mathbb C(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\boldsymbol x).
\]
Thus, Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_field} first determines how the imposed macroscopic
strain is redistributed within the matrix and fibers, while
\(\mathbb C\) converts that local strain into the corresponding local stress.
The same role is played by \(\mathbb A\) in the other linear problems listed
in Table~\ref{tab:general_physics}.

For a prescribed macroscopic loading \(g\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_field} gives
the local field as
\(\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)+\mathcal L u_g\), where \(u_g\) is the
microscopic fluctuation associated with that loading. The constitutive
operator \(\mathbb A\) converts this field into the corresponding local
response. The unknown \(u_g\) is then determined by requiring this response
to satisfy microscopic equilibrium. In weak form, this condition is written
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:general_cell_problem}
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L v)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u_g
\right]
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x
=
0
\qquad
\forall v\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(v\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}\) is an admissible periodic test
function.

The physical meaning of Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem} is particularly
clear in linear elasticity. In this case,
\(\mathcal L=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\) and
\(\mathbb A=\mathbb C\). Thus, the term in brackets is the local strain,
\(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
=\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(u_g)\), while
\(\mathbb C\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) is the local stress.
Equation~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem} therefore reduces to
\[
\int_{\Omega}
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(v)^T
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x
=
0,
\]
which is the weak form of mechanical equilibrium and, in the absence of body
forces, corresponds to \(\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0\). Hence, for
known \(\G\), \(\boldsymbol\xi\), and \(g\),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem} determines the microscopic fluctuation
\(u_g\) required to satisfy equilibrium.


Once the equilibrium fluctuation \(u_g\) has been determined from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem}, the detailed microscopic response can be
replaced by an equivalent macroscopic operator. This operator is defined by
requiring the homogeneous representation to reproduce the minimum energy of
the heterogeneous periodic cell. Accordingly, the effective operator is
defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:effective_energy_definition}
\frac{1}{2}
g^T\Heff(\boldsymbol{\xi};\G)g
=
\min_{u\in\mathcal V_{\mathrm{per}}}
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u
\right]^T
\mathbb A
\left[
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
+
\mathcal L u
\right]
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol x.
\end{equation}
The minimizer in Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} is the equilibrium
fluctuation \(u_g\) obtained from Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem}. Thus,
\(\Heff\) replaces the resolved heterogeneous cell by a homogeneous operator
with the same macroscopic energy.

In linear elasticity, for example, \(\Heff=C^{\mathrm{eff}}\), and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} equates the macroscopic elastic
energy to the minimum strain energy stored in the heterogeneous cell. The
corresponding quantities for the other linear problems considered here are
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:general_physics}.

For transport laws conventionally written with a minus sign, such as Fourier,
Fick, or Darcy laws, the sign is absorbed into the definition of the
macroscopic driving so that \(\Heff\) remains positive definite.

\subsection{Discrete energy and effective Schur map}
\label{subsec:schur_map}

Equation~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} is now converted from its
continuous form into a finite-dimensional algebraic problem. After spatial
discretization, the fluctuation field \(u(\boldsymbol x)\) is represented by
a vector \(u\in\R^n\), where \(n\) is the number of microscopic degrees of
freedom. Expanding the discretized quadratic energy separates three
contributions: the microscopic fluctuation energy, its coupling with the
macroscopic loading, and the direct macroscopic energy. These contributions
define the blocks \(K\), \(B\), and \(D\), respectively. The resulting
discrete energy is written in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:discrete_energy}
\mathcal E(u,g;\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\frac{1}{2}u^T K(\boldsymbol\xi)u
-
u^T B(\boldsymbol\xi)g
+
\frac{1}{2}g^T D(\boldsymbol\xi)g.
\end{equation}
Here, \(K\in\R^{n\times n}\) governs the microscopic response,
\(B\in\R^{n\times m}\) couples the microscopic fluctuation to the macroscopic
loading, and \(D\in\R^{m\times m}\) contains the direct macroscopic energy
contribution. In linear elasticity, this corresponds to discretizing the
strain energy associated with
\(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
=\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(u)\), with \(K\) playing the role of the microscopic
stiffness operator. The complete expansion from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}.

The equilibrium fluctuation minimizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} with
respect to \(u\). With \(B\) defined as the negative cross-energy block, the
corresponding stationarity condition is given in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stationarity}
K(\boldsymbol\xi)u
=
B(\boldsymbol\xi)g.
\end{equation}

Rather than solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} separately for every
macroscopic loading, the \(m\) independent unit loading states introduced in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field} are solved together. Their microscopic responses
are collected in the matrix
\(X=[x_1,\ldots,x_m]\in\R^{n\times m}\). The resulting full-order response
problem is given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fom_response}
\boxed{
K(\boldsymbol\xi)X(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
B(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
Each column of \(X\) therefore represents the microscopic fluctuation produced
by one independent macroscopic loading state. Consequently,
\(X=K^{-1}B\), and the response to an arbitrary loading vector is \(u=Xg\).

Eliminating the microscopic variables from Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy}
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} yields the discrete effective operator. The
complete algebraic condensation is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}; its result is the Schur map given in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schur_transfer}
\boxed{
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B(\boldsymbol\xi)^T X(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B(\boldsymbol\xi)^T
K(\boldsymbol\xi)^{-1}
B(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} condenses the high-dimensional microscopic
response into the \(m\times m\) effective operator that is ultimately required
from homogenization. Although \(X\) may contain millions of microscopic
degrees of freedom, the final effective operator remains small; for example,
\(m=6\) in three-dimensional linear elasticity.


\subsection{Geometry--material affine separation}
\label{subsec:affine_separation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} expresses the effective response in terms of
the discrete blocks \(K\), \(B\), and \(D\). However, these blocks still depend
on the queried constitutive properties \(\boldsymbol\xi\). Therefore, changing
the material would still modify the full-order operators even though the
microstructure \(\G\) remains unchanged.

To isolate the variable material dependence from the fixed microstructure, we
require the local constitutive operator to admit a finite affine separation.
Accordingly, the operator
\(\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)\), which enters the cell problem
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_cell_problem} and the energy definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition}, is written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_affine}
\boxed{
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G).
}
\end{equation}
Here, \(Q\) is the number of affine terms,
\(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) contains only the queried constitutive
dependence, whereas \(\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)\) contains the fixed
spatial information, including the phase distribution, interfaces, and local
orientations. Thus, Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} separates what changes with
the material from what remains fixed with the microstructure.

In linear elasticity, for example, the coefficients
\(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) are obtained from the elastic properties of the
matrix and fibers, whereas the fields \(\mathbb A_q\) encode their spatial
distribution and local fiber orientations. For the constitutive family used
in this work, this separation is exact; its elasticity-specific construction
is given in Appendix~\ref{app:affine}.

Because the discrete forms defining \(K\), \(B\), and \(D\) are linear in
\(\mathbb A\), substitution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} produces the
affine discrete operators collected in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:affine_operators}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
K(\boldsymbol\xi)&=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_q(\G),
&\quad
B(\boldsymbol\xi)&=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_q(\G),
&\quad
D(\boldsymbol\xi)&=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)D_q(\G).
\end{aligned}}
\end{equation}
The derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:affine_discrete_derivation}. The same material coefficients
\(\gamma_q\) therefore assemble the three discrete blocks required by the
Schur map in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer}.

For a fixed microstructure, the reusable geometry-dependent data are therefore
collected as
\(\mathcal K_{\G}=\{K_q(\G),B_q(\G),D_q(\G)\}_{q=1}^{Q}\) and computed once.
A new material query changes only
\(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi)
=[\gamma_1(\boldsymbol\xi),\ldots,\gamma_Q(\boldsymbol\xi)]^T\).

Thus, Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} connects the Schur representation in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} with the geometry--material separation: the
fixed microstructure is compiled once in \(\mathcal K_{\G}\), whereas each
new material enters only through \(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi)\).
Changing \(\G\), however, requires a new offline compilation.

\section{Sobol--POD transfer operator}
\label{sec:transfer}

The affine separation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} removes the need to
rebuild the fixed geometry for each material query, but the full-order problem
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} still scales with the microscopic dimension
\(n\). This section reduces that solve with a Sobol-sampled POD/Ritz space and
validates the resulting effective operator on independent full-order
materials.

\subsection{Reduced transfer operator}
\label{subsec:reduced_transfer}

A low-dimensional response space is first constructed from full-order
solutions sampled over the admissible constitutive domain \(\Xi\).

Let
\(\Xi_S=\{\boldsymbol\xi^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol\xi^{(N_s)}\}\subset\Xi\)
denote the prescribed initial Sobol set, where \(N_s\) is the number of
sampled materials. For each \(\boldsymbol\xi^{(j)}\),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} is solved for the \(m\) independent macroscopic
loading states, giving
\(X(\boldsymbol\xi^{(j)})\in\R^{n\times m}\). Since each material contributes
\(m\) microscopic response columns, all solutions are concatenated into the
snapshot matrix defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:snapshot_matrix},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snapshot_matrix}
S
=
\left[
X(\boldsymbol\xi^{(1)}),\ldots,
X(\boldsymbol\xi^{(N_s)})
\right]
\in\R^{n\times mN_s}.
\end{equation}

The independent response directions contained in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:snapshot_matrix} are identified through the singular value
decomposition
\[
S=U\Sigma W^T,
\]
where the columns of \(U\) are orthonormal microscopic response modes,
\(\Sigma\) contains their singular values, and \(W\) contains the
corresponding snapshot coefficients. The SVD is used here only to identify
and orthonormalize the numerically independent directions already present in
the snapshot space.

Let \(\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\) denote the singular values in
\(\Sigma\). The number of numerically independent response directions is
defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:numerical_rank} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:numerical_rank}
r
=
\#\left\{
i:
\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_1}
>
\tau_{\mathrm{rank}}
\right\},
\end{equation}
where \(\tau_{\mathrm{rank}}\) is the prescribed relative numerical-rank
tolerance. The corresponding reduced POD/Ritz basis is defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fullrank_pod} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fullrank_pod}
\boxed{
V_r=U(:,1:r).
}
\end{equation}
Thus, \(V_r\in\R^{n\times r}\) contains the \(r\) orthonormal microscopic
response modes retained from the snapshots. POD therefore introduces neither
a prescribed ROM dimension nor an additional energy-retention threshold; it
retains the complete numerically resolved snapshot span.

For a new material \(\boldsymbol\xi\), the microscopic response is
approximated as a combination of the modes in \(V_r\). This reduced
representation is defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_state} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced_state}
X_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
V_r\Xihat(\boldsymbol\xi),
\qquad
\Xihat(\boldsymbol\xi)\in\R^{r\times m}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(X_r\in\R^{n\times m}\) is the reconstructed microscopic response,
whereas \(\Xihat\) contains its coordinates in the reduced basis \(V_r\).

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_state} into the full-order problem of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response} and enforcing Galerkin orthogonality gives
\[
V_r^T
\left(
K V_r\Xihat-B
\right)
=
0.
\]
Defining \(K_r=V_r^TKV_r\) and \(B_r=V_r^TB\), the reduced problem is therefore
given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response} by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced_response}
\boxed{
K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)\Xihat(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}
Since \(K_r\in\R^{r\times r}\) and \(r\ll n\), the original
\(n\)-dimensional microscopic solve is replaced by a much smaller dense
system.

The geometry--material separation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} is next
projected onto the same reduced basis. For example,
\[
K_r
=
V_r^T
\left(
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q K_q
\right)
V_r
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q
\left(
V_r^TK_qV_r
\right),
\]
with an analogous expression for \(B_r\). This motivates the
geometry-dependent reduced blocks defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:compiled_reduced_operators},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:compiled_reduced_operators}
K_{q,r}=V_r^TK_qV_r,
\qquad
B_{q,r}=V_r^TB_q,
\end{equation}
which are computed once during the offline stage.

A new material then enters only through the constitutive coefficients
\(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\). The resulting online assembly is given in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:online_assembly}
K_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_{q,r},
\qquad
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_{q,r}.
\end{equation}
The block \(D(\boldsymbol\xi)\) requires no projection because it already has
dimension \(m\times m\); it is assembled directly from its affine form in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}.

Finally, combining the reduced representation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_state}
with \(B_r=V_r^TB\) gives \(B^TX_r=B_r^T\Xihat\). Hence, the reduced model
preserves the same Schur structure as the full-order operator in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer}. The reduced transfer operator is therefore
defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced_transfer}
\boxed{
\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
-
B_r(\boldsymbol\xi)^T
\Xihat(\boldsymbol\xi).
}
\end{equation}

The Ritz construction also preserves a variational ordering of the effective
operator. For a fixed material point, define the microscopic response error
\(E_X=X-X_r\) and the full-order residual \(R=B-KX_r\). Since \(K\) is
symmetric positive definite on the constrained periodic space and \(X_r\) is
the Galerkin solution, the reduced and full-order Schur operators satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_schur_identity}
\boxed{
\Hr-\Hfom
=
E_X^T K E_X
=
R^T K^{-1}R
\succeq 0.
}
\end{equation}
Thus, the ROM--FOM difference is exactly the microscopic Ritz error measured
in the \(K\)-energy and the reduced operator approaches the full-order Schur
map from above in the Loewner sense. Moreover, for any two nested trial spaces
\(\operatorname{span}(V_r)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(V_s)\), their effective
operators satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}
\boxed{
\Hfom
\preceq
\Hs
\preceq
\Hr.
}
\end{equation}
The derivation of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity} and
\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering} is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:ritz_schur}. The nested-space statement is a structural
property of Ritz approximation. The implementation retains every accepted
basis vector and only appends numerically independent directions, so its trial
spaces are nested by construction. Consequently, in exact arithmetic the
positive-semidefinite error in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity}, its
Frobenius norm, and the corresponding maximum over a fixed monitor set are
nonincreasing. Mixed precision, iterative-solver tolerances, and finite
orthogonalization accuracy can introduce small numerical perturbations to this
ordering; monotonicity is therefore audited rather than assumed bitwise.

A standard material query therefore requires only the constitutive
coefficients \(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\), the assembly of the small reduced
operators, and the solution of the \(r\times r\) system in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response}. No full \(n\)-dimensional solve is required.
\subsection{Adaptive FFT monitoring and held-out validation}
\label{subsec:heldout_validation}

Materials used to control enrichment must be separated from those used for
the final assessment. Let
\(\Xi_M=\{\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol\xi_M^{(N_M)}\}\) denote
a fixed monitor set and let
\(\Xi_V=\{\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol\xi_V^{(N_V)}\}\) denote
a second independent final-validation set. Neither set contributes snapshots.
For a material \(\boldsymbol\xi_A^{(j)}\), where
\(A\in\{M,V\}\), the relative tensor error is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:validation_error}
e_{A,j}
=
\frac{
\left\|
\Hr(\boldsymbol\xi_A^{(j)})
-
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_A^{(j)})
\right\|_F
}{
\left\|
\Hfom(\boldsymbol\xi_A^{(j)})
\right\|_F
}.
\end{equation}
The \(N_M=16\) full-order monitor tensors are computed once before enrichment.
Starting at the second Sobol training material, the current ROM is evaluated
on \(\Xi_M\) after every newly appended material. Enrichment stops when
\(\max_j e_{M,j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ROM}}\) for the first time. The
training sequence remains Sobol ordered; the monitor set determines only when
its prefix is long enough. For the elasticity campaign,
\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ROM}}=10^{-4}\).

After stopping, the reduced operators are frozen. Only then are the
\(N_V=16\) final-validation materials solved with the full-order model and
evaluated once with the ROM. The reported final statistics are the mean,
median, 95th percentile, and maximum of \(e_{V,j}\). In addition, for
\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rep}}=10^{-4}\), the reported empirical coverage is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:validation_coverage}
N_{\mathrm{pass}}
=
\sum_{j=1}^{N_V}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[e_{V,j}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rep}}\right],
\qquad
\widehat p_{\mathrm{pass}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{pass}}}{N_V}.
\end{equation}
The threshold \(10^{-4}\) corresponds to \(0.01\%\) relative Frobenius error
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:validation_error}. It is a tensor-norm criterion, not a
componentwise bound. Because \(\Xi_V\) is finite and is not examined during
enrichment, \(\widehat p_{\mathrm{pass}}\) is an independent empirical result,
not a certificate over the continuous parameter domain.

\subsection{Offline--online summary}
\label{subsec:offline_online}

The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:offline_online}.
The offline stage contains every operation involving the full microscopic
dimension, whereas a standard online query uses only the reduced affine
operators.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Sobol--POD transfer operator for one fixed microstructure
\(\G\).}
\label{alg:offline_online}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Initial offline compilation.}
\item Fix \(\G\) and assemble
      \(\{K_q,B_q,D_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}.
\item Generate a fixed Sobol training sequence and independent monitor and
      final-validation sets \(\Xi_M\) and \(\Xi_V\).
\item Solve the \(N_M=16\) monitor materials once with the full-order model.
\item Traverse the Sobol sequence one material at a time: solve the new
      material, append its \(m\) responses to \(S\), retain the full
      numerical-rank basis \(V_r\), and update the projected blocks in
      Eq.~\eqref{eq:compiled_reduced_operators}.
\item From the second training material onward, evaluate
      \(\max_j e_{M,j}\) after every update. Stop at the first prefix at or
      below \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ROM}}=10^{-4}\).
\item Freeze the final reduced operators and discard the microscopic snapshots
      and POD basis.
\item Solve the \(N_V=16\) final-validation materials once, evaluate
      Eq.~\eqref{eq:validation_error}, and report the final error statistics
      together with the observed count and percentage below \(10^{-4}\).
\item \textbf{Online material query.}
\item Given \(\boldsymbol\xi^\star\), evaluate
      \(\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi^\star)\).
\item Assemble \(K_r\) and \(B_r\) using Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly},
      assemble \(D\), and solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response}.
\item Evaluate \(\Hr\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_transfer}.
\item Return the reduced effective operator \(\Hr\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Computational scaling and bounded workspaces}
\label{subsec:computational_scaling}

The complete numerical snapshot span is retained, and therefore the reduced
rank is bounded by \(r\leq mN_s\) but is not prescribed independently of the
Sobol budget. Let \(n\) denote the number of entries in one microscopic
response vector. In the voxel elasticity implementation,
\(n=6N_{\mathrm{vox}}\). Exact storage of the basis is
\(\mathcal O(nr)\), while block orthonormalization and incremental compilation
of all affine Ritz matrices require
\(\mathcal O(nr^2)\) and \(\mathcal O(Qnr^2)\) cumulative work, respectively.
The compiled operators require \(\mathcal O(Qr^2+Qrm)\) memory.

For one online material, affine assembly costs \(\mathcal O(Qr^2)\), and the
dense reduced solve with \(m\) right-hand sides costs
\(\mathcal O(r^3+r^2m)\). A batch of \(c\) materials consequently requires an
\(\mathcal O(cr^2)\) temporary workspace. These scalings are summarized as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:computational_scaling}
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{basis}}=\mathcal O(nr),
\qquad
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{offline}}=\mathcal O(Qnr^2),
\qquad
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{online}}=\mathcal O(r^3).
\end{equation}

The implementation bounds temporary memory without modifying the reduced
space. Snapshot columns are orthonormalized in memory-limited blocks, the
\(Q\) affine stress actions are compiled in coefficient blocks, and the online
batch size decreases proportionally to \(r^{-2}\). The snapshot projection is
performed as an in-place BLAS matrix product. As the basis occupies more host
memory, the snapshot and coefficient blocks are reduced automatically. Full
voxel fields and the POD basis are transient: after the reduced operators and
validation data have been generated, they are not retained in the final run
directory. A preflight memory estimate rejects an exact full-rank request
before allocation when even the minimum workspaces would exceed the prescribed
fraction of available host memory. Online batches retain the affine operators
in double precision on a CUDA device when available, with a double-precision
CPU fallback.
Reducing the asymptotic dependence on \(r\) would require rank truncation or a
different reduced solver and would therefore define a different approximation
than the full numerical-rank method studied here.


\section{Elasticity specialization}
\label{sec:elasticity}

The general formulation is now specialized to three-dimensional linear
elasticity. No change is made to the transfer construction itself. The
specialization is stated in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_specialization} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elastic_specialization}
m=6,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}u
=
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(u),
\qquad
\mathbb{A}
=
\mathbb{C},
\qquad
\Heff
=
\Ceff.
\end{equation}
Accordingly, \(X\in\R^{n\times6}\),
\(\Hfom=\Cfom\), and \(\Hr=\Cr\); the Schur map of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} becomes
\(\Cfom=D-B^TK^{-1}B\).

For the constitutive family considered here, the matrix phase is isotropic
and the fibers are transversely isotropic. The queried constitutive vector is
defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_material_vector} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elastic_material_vector}
\boldsymbol{\xi}
=
(E_m,\nu_m,E_L,E_T,G_{LT},\nu_{LT},\nu_{TT}),
\end{equation}
which gives two isotropic matrix coefficients and five transversely isotropic
fiber coefficients, hence \(Q=7\). Their construction is summarized in
Appendix~\ref{app:affine}.

For each queried elastic material, the seven coefficients associated with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_material_vector} are inserted into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_assembly}. The reduced response in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response} then gives the ROM stiffness tensor
\(\Cr\). Independent full-order FFT solves provide \(\Cfom\) for held-out
materials, and the tensor error is measured with Eq.~\eqref{eq:validation_error}.


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A common Sobol--POD transfer formulation has been developed for
repeated periodic homogenization with fixed geometry and varying local
constitutive properties. The construction combines a Schur representation of
the effective operator, an affine geometry--material separation, and a
numerical-rank POD Ritz approximation of the microscopic response matrix.
The Ritz restriction preserves the variational structure of the Schur map:
the ROM--FOM operator difference is positive semidefinite and is exactly the
\(K\)-energy of the microscopic response error. For nested reduced spaces,
the effective operators are therefore ordered monotonically toward the
full-order operator.

The formulation is not restricted to elasticity. It applies directly to
linear, symmetric, and coercive periodic cell problems such as thermal and
electrical conduction, mass diffusion, dielectric electrostatics, linear
Darcy transport, and linear elasticity whenever the local constitutive law
admits the required finite separable representation. The number of
macroscopic loadings changes with the physics, but the transfer algebra
remains unchanged.

Geometry is handled implicitly through the full-order operators and snapshots
used by the SVD. Consequently, no morphology-specific reduced descriptor is
required. The framework is independent of the geometry class, while each
compiled operator remains specific to the fixed geometry from which it was
constructed.

The adaptive assessment separates model construction from final evaluation.
A fixed full-order monitor set controls when Sobol enrichment stops, while a
second independent full-order set is evaluated only after the reduced
operators have been frozen. The reported relative Frobenius errors therefore
separate stopping from final held-out assessment while keeping the protocol
reproducible.

The three-dimensional elasticity specialization illustrates how the abstract
operators become stiffness tensors, Mandel rotations, and seven affine
constitutive coefficients. Extensions to the scalar
transport problems listed in Table~\ref{tab:general_physics} require only the
corresponding local constitutive operator, macroscopic loading basis, affine
coefficient map, and validation protocol. Mixed pore-scale flow and
nonlinear problems require additional stability or nonlinear reduction
machinery and remain outside the direct scope of the present formulation.

\section{Derivation of the discrete Schur representation}
\label{app:schur_derivation}

Starting from the effective-energy definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition}, expansion of the quadratic
integrand separates the microscopic, coupling, and macroscopic contributions,
\[
\mathcal E
=
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L u)^T\mathbb A(\mathcal L u)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x
+
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L u)^T\mathbb A\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x
+
\frac{1}{2}
\int_{\Omega}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)^T
\mathbb A
\overline{\boldsymbol z}(g)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x.
\]

After spatial discretization, write
\(u(\boldsymbol x)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}u_a\phi_a(\boldsymbol x)\).
Using the macroscopic loading expansion in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro_field}, the
three discrete blocks are
\begin{align}
K_{ab}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\\
B_{ai}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
-
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\\
D_{ij}(\boldsymbol\xi)
&=
\int_{\Omega}
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_i^T
\mathbb A(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
\overline{\boldsymbol z}_j
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\end{align}
with \(a,b=1,\ldots,n\) and \(i,j=1,\ldots,m\). These definitions give the
discrete energy in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy}; the minus sign in \(B\)
accounts for the cross-energy convention used there.

Stationarity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete_energy} with respect to \(u\) gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity}. Collecting the \(m\) unit-load responses then
gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:fom_response}, so \(X=K^{-1}B\) and \(u=Xg\).
Substitution into the discrete energy yields
\[
\mathcal E(Xg,g)
=
\frac{1}{2}g^TX^TKXg
-
g^TX^TBg
+
\frac{1}{2}g^TDg.
\]
Using \(KX=B\) and \(K=K^T\) gives
\(X^TKX=X^TB=B^TX\), and therefore
\[
\mathcal E(Xg,g)
=
\frac{1}{2}
g^T
\left[
D-B^TX
\right]
g.
\]
Comparison with Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_energy_definition} proves the Schur
representation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer}.


\section{Derivation of the affine discrete operators}
\label{app:affine_discrete_derivation}

The discrete affine operators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators} follow from
the local separation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} and the block definitions
in Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation}. For example, substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} into the definition of \(K_{ab}\) gives
\[
K_{ab}(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x.
\]
Because \(\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)\) is independent of
\(\boldsymbol x\), the remaining integral defines
\[
(K_q)_{ab}
=
\int_{\Omega}
(\mathcal L\phi_a)^T
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)
(\mathcal L\phi_b)
\,\mathrm d\boldsymbol x,
\]
and hence
\(K(\boldsymbol\xi)=\sum_q\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)K_q(\G)\).

Applying the same substitution to the \(B\) and \(D\) definitions in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur_derivation} gives
\[
B(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)B_q(\G),
\qquad
D(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)D_q(\G),
\]
which completes the derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}.


\section{Ritz--Schur identity and variational ordering}
\label{app:ritz_schur}

This appendix proves the structure-preserving property stated in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity} and
\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}. Consider one fixed material point
\(\boldsymbol\xi\) and suppress its dependence for readability. Let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_error_residual}
E_X=X-X_r,
\qquad
R=B-KX_r.
\end{equation}
Using the full-order equation \(KX=B\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_error_relation}
R
=
KX-KX_r
=
K E_X.
\end{equation}
Since \(K\) is invertible on the constrained periodic space,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:error_from_residual}
E_X=K^{-1}R.
\end{equation}

From the full-order and reduced Schur maps,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:schur_transfer} and \eqref{eq:reduced_transfer},
\begin{align}
\Hr-\Hfom
&=
\left(D-B^TX_r\right)
-
\left(D-B^TX\right)
\nonumber\\
&=
B^T(X-X_r)
=
B^TE_X.
\label{eq:schur_difference_first}
\end{align}
Because \(B=KX=K(X_r+E_X)\) and \(K=K^T\),
\begin{align}
B^TE_X
&=
(X_r+E_X)^T K E_X
\nonumber\\
&=
X_r^T K E_X
+
E_X^T K E_X.
\label{eq:schur_difference_expand}
\end{align}

The Galerkin equation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_response} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:galerkin_residual_orthogonality}
V_r^T(KX_r-B)=0,
\end{equation}
or, with the residual definition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_error_residual},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:galerkin_residual_zero}
V_r^TR=0.
\end{equation}
Using \(X_r=V_r\widehat X_r\) and
\(K E_X=R\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual_error_relation},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cross_term_zero}
X_r^T K E_X
=
\widehat X_r^T V_r^T R
=
0.
\end{equation}
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur_difference_expand} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_energy_identity_appendix}
\Hr-\Hfom
=
E_X^T K E_X.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_from_residual} and the symmetry of \(K\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_energy_identity_appendix}
E_X^T K E_X
=
R^T K^{-1}R.
\end{equation}
Finally, for any \(g\in\R^m\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ritz_psd_proof}
g^T(\Hr-\Hfom)g
=
(E_Xg)^T K(E_Xg)
\geq 0,
\end{equation}
because \(K\) is positive definite. Therefore,
\[
\Hr-\Hfom\succeq0,
\]
which proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:ritz_schur_identity}.

The nested-space ordering follows directly from the same variational
structure. For a fixed macroscopic loading \(g\), the full-order solution
minimizes the discrete energy over the complete admissible microscopic space,
whereas the Ritz solutions minimize the same energy over their respective
trial spaces. Thus,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:restricted_energy_minima}
\frac{1}{2}g^T\Hfom g
=
\min_{u\in\R^n}\mathcal E(u,g),
\qquad
\frac{1}{2}g^T\Hr g
=
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_r)}\mathcal E(u,g),
\end{equation}
with the understood periodic constraint already incorporated in the discrete
space. If
\(\operatorname{span}(V_r)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(V_s)\), then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nested_minima}
\min_{u\in\R^n}\mathcal E(u,g)
\leq
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_s)}\mathcal E(u,g)
\leq
\min_{u\in\operatorname{span}(V_r)}\mathcal E(u,g).
\end{equation}
Hence, for every \(g\in\R^m\),
\[
g^T\Hfom g
\leq
g^T\Hs g
\leq
g^T\Hr g,
\]
which is equivalent to the Loewner ordering
\[
\Hfom
\preceq
\Hs
\preceq
\Hr.
\]
This proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested_ritz_ordering}. The result concerns genuinely
nested trial spaces. When POD bases are recomputed at successive Sobol
prefixes with a relative numerical-rank threshold, exact nestedness is not
required and is not assumed by the adaptive monitoring procedure.


\section{Affine constitutive coefficients for the elasticity specialization}
\label{app:affine}

This appendix specializes the general affine separation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_affine} to the elastic material family defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_material_vector}. Let
\(\chi_m(\boldsymbol x)\) and \(\chi_f(\boldsymbol x)\) denote the matrix and
fiber indicator functions, with \(\chi_m+\chi_f=1\).

For the isotropic matrix, the Lam\'e parameters are
\[
\mu_m
=
\frac{E_m}{2(1+\nu_m)},
\qquad
\lambda_m
=
\frac{E_m\nu_m}{(1+\nu_m)(1-2\nu_m)}.
\]
The corresponding Mandel stiffness is represented by the two fixed basis
matrices in Eq.~\eqref{eq:matrix_affine_basis},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:matrix_affine_basis}
C_m
=
\lambda_m A^{(m)}_1
+
\mu_m A^{(m)}_2
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_m+2\mu_m & \lambda_m & \lambda_m & 0 & 0 & 0\\
\lambda_m & \lambda_m+2\mu_m & \lambda_m & 0 & 0 & 0\\
\lambda_m & \lambda_m & \lambda_m+2\mu_m & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0&0&0&2\mu_m&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&2\mu_m&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&2\mu_m
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The fiber is transversely isotropic about its local longitudinal axis. From
the five independent engineering constants in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_material_vector},
\[
G_{TT}
=
\frac{E_T}{2(1+\nu_{TT})},
\qquad
\nu_{TL}
=
\nu_{LT}\frac{E_T}{E_L}.
\]
Using the Mandel ordering
\((11,22,33,\sqrt{2}\,23,\sqrt{2}\,13,\sqrt{2}\,12)\), the local fiber
compliance is defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fiber_local_compliance} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fiber_local_compliance}
S_f^{\mathrm{loc}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{1}{E_L}
&
-\dfrac{\nu_{LT}}{E_L}
&
-\dfrac{\nu_{LT}}{E_L}
&
0&0&0
\\[2mm]
-\dfrac{\nu_{LT}}{E_L}
&
\dfrac{1}{E_T}
&
-\dfrac{\nu_{TT}}{E_T}
&
0&0&0
\\[2mm]
-\dfrac{\nu_{LT}}{E_L}
&
-\dfrac{\nu_{TT}}{E_T}
&
\dfrac{1}{E_T}
&
0&0&0
\\[2mm]
0&0&0&
\dfrac{1}{2G_{TT}}&0&0
\\[2mm]
0&0&0&0&
\dfrac{1}{2G_{LT}}&0
\\[2mm]
0&0&0&0&0&
\dfrac{1}{2G_{LT}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The local stiffness is
\(C_f^{\mathrm{loc}}=(S_f^{\mathrm{loc}})^{-1}\). Transverse isotropy gives
the five-coefficient representation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fiber_local_stiffness},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fiber_local_stiffness}
C_f^{\mathrm{loc}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1 & c_2 & c_2 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
c_2 & c_3 & c_4 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
c_2 & c_4 & c_3 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & c_3-c_4 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & c_5 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & c_5
\end{bmatrix}
=
\sum_{j=1}^{5}
c_j(\boldsymbol\xi)A^{(f)}_j.
\end{equation}
The coefficients \(c_j(\boldsymbol\xi)\) follow directly from the inverse of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fiber_local_compliance}; the matrices \(A_j^{(f)}\) are fixed.

Let \(T(\boldsymbol x;\G)\) denote the energy-preserving Mandel rotation
associated with the local fiber direction. Combining the matrix and fiber
phases gives the local stiffness field defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_local_field},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elastic_local_field}
\mathbb C(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
=
\chi_m(\boldsymbol x)C_m
+
\chi_f(\boldsymbol x)
T(\boldsymbol x;\G)
C_f^{\mathrm{loc}}
T(\boldsymbol x;\G)^T.
\end{equation}

Substituting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:matrix_affine_basis} and
\eqref{eq:fiber_local_stiffness} into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_local_field} gives the exact elasticity-specific
separation defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_affine_expansion},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elastic_affine_expansion}
\mathbb C(\boldsymbol x;\boldsymbol\xi,\G)
=
\sum_{q=1}^{7}
\gamma_q(\boldsymbol\xi)
\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G),
\end{equation}
The corresponding coefficient vector is defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_gamma} as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elastic_gamma}
\boldsymbol\gamma(\boldsymbol\xi)
=
\left[
\lambda_m,\,
\mu_m,\,
c_1,\,
c_2,\,
c_3,\,
c_4,\,
c_5
\right]^T,
\qquad
Q=7.
\end{equation}
The associated fixed spatial fields are
\[
\mathbb A_1
=
\chi_m A^{(m)}_1,
\qquad
\mathbb A_2
=
\chi_m A^{(m)}_2,
\]
and, for \(j=1,\ldots,5\),
\[
\mathbb A_{2+j}(\boldsymbol x;\G)
=
\chi_f(\boldsymbol x)
T(\boldsymbol x;\G)
A^{(f)}_j
T(\boldsymbol x;\G)^T.
\]
Thus, the queried material changes only the seven coefficients in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elastic_gamma}; the phase distribution and local orientations
remain fixed in \(\mathbb A_q(\boldsymbol x;\G)\). These fields generate the
reusable discrete blocks in Eq.~\eqref{eq:affine_operators}.
